Gratitude and the Teachers Who Pass Music On

At this time of year I think about the teachers behind my playing, and about how an adult learning alone can still have teachers through careful listening to great recordings.
By Dr. Maria Pisarenko, DMA  ·  November 26, 2026

Taught by Dr. Maria Pisarenko, DMA — the complete beginner course is free: Start at Lesson 1 →

The season of Thanksgiving is a natural time to think about where things come from. For a pianist, that question has an unusual answer. Almost nothing we know about playing the piano was learned from a book. It was learned from a person, who learned it from another person, and so on back through generations. Piano playing is passed on by hand.

How playing is passed on

A teacher does not only correct wrong notes. A teacher shows how to sit, how the arm should feel, how to listen to the end of a sound, how a phrase should breathe. Much of this cannot be written down. It is shown, imitated, corrected and shown again. Over years, a pupil absorbs not just information but a way of hearing.

That is why pianists speak of lineage. When a teacher passes on what they learned, they also pass on what their own teachers valued: a certain kind of tone, a certain seriousness about the score, a certain way of practicing.

My own teachers

I trained at the Central Music School of the Moscow Tchaikovsky Conservatory and later at the Gnessin Academy. My formative teacher was Manana Kandelaki, who was herself a pupil of Tatiana Nikolayeva, one of the great Russian pianists and a renowned interpreter of Bach. Nikolayeva, in turn, studied with Alexander Goldenweiser at the Moscow Conservatory.

I am grateful for that chain. When I teach, I am aware that some of what I say was first said in a classroom long before I existed. The habits of careful listening, of respect for the composer’s text and of patient work on sound did not begin with me. They were given to me, and my work is to pass them on clearly and honestly.

But what if you are learning alone?

Many adults who come to the piano at forty or fifty or sixty are learning on their own. They may not have a teacher nearby, or the time, or the budget. It is easy to feel that without a teacher you are outside this tradition. You are not.

Great pianists have left us something earlier generations could never have: recordings. You can hear how a master shapes a melody, how they let a chord ring, how they make a quiet passage sound alive. Listening carefully to recordings is not the same as a lesson, but it is a real form of learning. In a sense, the great players can become your teachers, too.

How to learn from a recording

Listening as a student is different from listening as an audience member. Here is how I suggest you do it.

  • Listen to something short. One minute is enough. A slow piece or a slow movement is best, because you can hear every decision.
  • Listen for one thing at a time. First, only the melody: where does it get louder, where does it soften? Then only the bass. Then only the silences between phrases.
  • Compare two pianists. Choose the same piece played by two different artists. The differences will show you that music is made of choices, and that there is more than one honest way to play.
  • Then go to your piano. Take one idea, a phrase that tapers, a note held a little longer, and try it in something you can already play.

Gratitude as a practice habit

There is a practical side to gratitude. When we remember that this music was handed down with great care, we treat it with more care ourselves. We practice more slowly. We listen more closely. We stop rushing past the beautiful moments to get to the end.

For an adult learning alone, this attitude matters even more. You are your own teacher most of the time. Being a kind, patient and attentive teacher to yourself is part of joining the tradition.

What you can try this week

  • Choose one recording of a slow piano piece by a great pianist and listen to it three times, each time for something different: melody, bass, silence.
  • Write down one thing you noticed that you would like to try in your own playing.
  • Play one short phrase you know, slowly, and listen to the end of every note. Treat it as if someone handed it to you to look after.

Every pianist stands on the shoulders of teachers. Whether you have one in the room or only in your headphones, you are part of that line now.
