How to Tell If You Are Actually Improving at the Piano

The things that improve first are not the things you are watching. That is why so many adults conclude they are stuck a month before it shows.
By Dr. Maria Pisarenko, DMA  ·  December 21, 2026

The free course is ordered so that each lesson builds on the last — progress you can see by where you are in it: Start the free course →

The most common thing an adult says to me after six months of teaching themselves is that they are not sure they are getting any better. Usually they are, substantially. The problem is that they are measuring the wrong things, and the right ones are invisible unless you look for them on purpose.

Why it is invisible from inside

Two reasons. The first is that your standard rises at exactly the same rate as your playing. The uneven note you did not hear in March is obvious to you in September — which feels like getting worse and is in fact your ear improving faster than your hands. That is the normal order and it never stops.

The second is that you only ever hear yourself at your current level. There is no before to compare with, because the before was not recorded.

The measures that mislead

How difficult the piece is. Tempting, and poor. Difficulty is not a straight line, and a beginner playing a simple piece beautifully has made far more progress than one stumbling through something hard.

How it felt today. Daily variation at the piano is enormous and has more to do with sleep and stress than with skill. A bad day is not a trend.

How you compare to someone online. This one only subtracts.

The measures that actually work

How long it takes you to learn something new. This is the honest one. Not how well you play the piece — how many days until it is playable. If a piece of a given level took you three weeks in the spring and takes eight days now, you have improved a great deal, whatever this afternoon sounded like.

How fast you recover. When something goes wrong mid-piece, what happens? Full stop in March, a small wobble and a continuation in December — that is a real, measurable gain, and it is the one that makes playing for people possible.

How much you have to look at your hands. Less looking means the hand knows where it is. Notice it over months.

What you can hear. Can you tell, now, when the melody is buried under the accompaniment? When a long note got clipped? Hearing faults is the skill that has to arrive before fixing them can, so a period where you notice more problems is a period of progress, however it feels.

Whether you can read something you have never seen. Open a piece from two lessons back that you never studied and read a line of it. That is a direct test of reading, unclouded by how many times you have repeated something.

Record yourself, once a month

The single most useful thing on this page. Once a month, put your phone on the piano and play something through, and keep the file. Do not listen to it — almost nobody enjoys hearing themselves at first, and that is fine, you are not making a recording for pleasure. You are making evidence.

In March, listen to January. The gap will be obvious and it will be nothing like what you expected: the notes will be about the same, and the sound will be entirely different. Steadier, rounder, more confident. That is what improvement actually looks like from outside, and it is the part you cannot perceive from the bench.

The plateau is usually not a plateau

Most of what adults call a plateau is a period in which the visible thing — how hard the piece is — stayed still while the invisible things kept moving: tone, reading, recovery, independence of the hands. Then, a few weeks later, a piece that should have taken a month takes a week, and it looks like a sudden jump.

It was not sudden. You simply could not see it while it was happening, and now you have a way to.
