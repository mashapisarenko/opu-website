What a Piano Teacher Hears in Your Playing That You Do Not

Adults learning alone check for wrong notes, because wrong notes are the only fault they have a name for. A teacher is listening to something else entirely.
By Dr. Maria Pisarenko, DMA  ·  December 23, 2026

Every lesson in the free course works on the sound, not only the notes: Start the free course →

When someone plays for me for the first time, they almost always apologize for the same thing afterwards: the wrong notes. And the wrong notes are usually the least interesting part of what I heard.

Not because accuracy does not matter, but because wrong notes are easy to fix and they are the only fault most self-taught adults have a name for. Here is what a trained ear is actually listening to, and how to start hearing it in your own playing — which you can, without a teacher, if you know what to listen for.

1. Whether the beat is yours or an accident

In the first bars I can tell whether the pulse is something you are producing or something that is happening to you.

It shows up in the relationship between difficulty and tempo: easy bars slightly fast, hard bars slightly slow, long notes clipped short. None of it dramatic. But a player who owns the beat sounds settled from the first note, and a player who does not sounds like they are being pulled along by the music.

How to hear it in yourself: count out loud while you play, and listen to your own voice rather than the piano. The voice keeps going steadily or it does not.

2. What your hand does after the key goes down

I watch for whether the hand releases. Adults concentrate, and concentration travels straight into the forearm — the note is played and the hand stays pressed into the key, holding weight it no longer needs.

You can hear it. The tone gets hard and slightly harsh, and it does not vary, because a locked arm can only deliver one kind of sound. Ninety percent of tone problems in beginner playing are this and nothing else.

How to hear it in yourself: play a single note and notice what your arm does in the second after. It should settle, like something being set down. If it stays pushed, that is the fault, and it is felt more easily than heard.

3. Whether the melody is on top

The moment two notes sound together, a question appears that beginners are rarely told about: which one is louder?

When both hands play at the same level the result is oddly flat — correct, unobjectionable, and dead. The ear does not know where to go. A teacher hears that instantly, and hears the opposite instantly too: the player who lets the tune come forward and the accompaniment sit underneath. That single balance is most of what people mean when they say someone plays musically.

How to hear it in yourself: record eight bars on your phone and listen once, only for this. It is much easier to hear in a recording than at the keyboard.

4. Where you are frightened

This is the one that surprises people. I can hear exactly which bar you are worried about, several bars before it arrives — because you slow down going into it, or you tighten, or you speed up to get it over with.

It is useful information rather than an embarrassment: it locates the place that needs work more accurately than you could by thinking about it. The bar you are frightened of is the bar to practice, small and slow, tomorrow morning.

How to hear it in yourself: the recording again. Listen for where your tempo bends. That is fear, and it is always in the same place.

None of these are talent

Every one of the four is a habit, and habits are trainable. What a teacher mostly provides is not knowledge — the knowledge is in the lessons and the books — but a second pair of ears, and a description of what they heard.

You can supply a good deal of that for yourself with a phone and the willingness to listen to one minute of your own playing a week, checking one thing at a time. Beat. Arm. Balance. Fear. Most adults who teach themselves check only for wrong notes, which is why their wrong notes disappear and their playing does not change.
