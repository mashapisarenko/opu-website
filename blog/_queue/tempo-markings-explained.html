Andante, Allegro, Moderato: What Tempo Markings Actually Mean

Beginners look up Allegro, find a number, and set the metronome. That is not quite what the word is for.
By Dr. Maria Pisarenko, DMA  ·  January 22, 2027

Tempo is taught as character, not as a number, throughout the free course: Start the free course →

At the top of the music, above the first bar, there is usually an Italian word. It tells you how fast — roughly — and, more usefully, what kind of piece this is.

From slowest to fastest, the ones you will actually meet:

Largo — broad, very slow.
Adagio — slow, at ease.
Andante — literally “walking.” A comfortable walking pace.
Moderato — moderate.
Allegro — the word means cheerful, not fast. Quick and bright.
Vivace — lively.
Presto — very fast.

Why the words are not numbers

Look up Allegro and you will find something like 120 to 168 beats per minute. That range is enormous — the top end is nearly half again as fast as the bottom — and that is not sloppiness. It is the point.

Allegro in a light two-page piece is not Allegro in a dense one with chords in both hands. The music itself decides where in the range it sits. The word tells you the character; the notes tell you the speed.

Notice how many of the words are descriptions of mood or motion rather than velocity. Andante is a walk. Allegro is cheerful. Largo is broad. Composers were describing how a piece behaves, and the tempo follows from that.

The modifiers

A few small words do most of the adjusting:

molto — very. poco — a little. non troppo — not too much. ma non troppo — but not too much. assai — quite.

So Allegro ma non troppo is “quick, but do not overdo it,” which is a composer’s way of saying: this will be tempting to rush, and it should not be rushed.

Changing speed inside a piece

Three you will see constantly, usually abbreviated:

rit. (ritardando) — gradually slowing.
accel. (accelerando) — gradually getting faster.
a tempo — back to the original speed, after one of the above.

And rubato, which means the pulse is allowed to stretch and recover expressively — a subject of its own, and best left until the piece is secure in strict time.

The practical part

Adults do two things with tempo markings and both cause trouble.

They set the metronome to the top of the range and try to get there. Then the piece is learned in a panic and never sounds settled. Choose the speed at which the hardest bar is comfortable, and let that be the tempo. A clean Andante is worth far more than a frantic Allegro.

They ignore the word entirely and play everything at one speed. The word is the first piece of information the composer gives you about what this music is. Read it before the first note, decide what kind of piece it is going to be, and start from there.

“Walking” is a real instruction. Played as one, an Andante immediately sounds like music instead of notes.
