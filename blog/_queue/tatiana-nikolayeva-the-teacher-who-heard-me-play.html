Tatiana Nikolayeva: The Teacher Who Heard Me Play

Dr. Pisarenko recalls playing for the pianist Tatiana Nikolayeva, whose pupil taught her, and shares practical steps for adults who dread playing for other people.
By Dr. Maria Pisarenko, DMA  ·  October 22, 2026

Taught by Dr. Maria Pisarenko, DMA — the complete beginner course is free: Start at Lesson 1 →

I have played for many teachers over the years, but one stands apart in my memory: Tatiana Nikolayeva. I played for her as a student, and I have never forgotten what it was to sit down at an instrument in front of someone of that stature. It is the best lesson I know on a subject many adult learners find harder than the notes themselves: playing for another person.

Who Tatiana Nikolayeva was

Tatiana Nikolayeva was one of the great Russian pianists of the last century. She studied at the Moscow Conservatory with Alexander Goldenweiser, and later she taught there herself, eventually as a professor. She is especially remembered for her playing of Bach and for her association with the Preludes and Fugues of Dmitri Shostakovich, which she was the first to perform in public. She was also a composer. A pianist who spent her life inside Bach’s counterpoint and Shostakovich’s fugues is a musician for whom every voice in a texture matters.

A quick note, because the names confuse many readers: this is Tatiana Nikolayeva, the pianist. She is not Leonid Nikolayev, the Leningrad teacher of Maria Yudina and Shostakovich, and she is not Alexander Alexandrovich Nikolaev, who wrote School of Piano Playing. Three different musicians, three similar-sounding names. I have taught from the work of all three, and I print their full names every time so that none of them gets lost.

How she fits into my story

My formative teacher at the Central Music School, Manana Kandelaki, was Tatiana Nikolayeva’s pupil. That meant I grew up hearing her ideas secondhand, through the way Manana Kandelaki listened and the things she asked me to notice. Playing for Tatiana Nikolayeva herself was the moment those secondhand ideas met their source.

I will not tell you what she said to me, because I would rather say nothing than polish a memory into a neat quotation. What I can tell you is what the occasion taught me, and it has shaped how I teach adults ever since.

What it taught me about playing for others

When you are about to play for someone you admire, your body does what everyone’s body does. Your breathing changes. Your hands feel unfamiliar. The piece you knew yesterday seems to belong to someone else. I do not think this ever fully disappears, even for professionals. What changes with experience is your relationship to it.

Here is what I learned, and what I pass on to my adult students:

  • Play for the music, not for the listener. The moment you start playing to be judged, you stop listening, and you lose the one thing that carries a performance. Put your attention on the sound, exactly as you do at home.
  • A serious teacher is listening to help. The people worth playing for want to hear what is there so they can show you what is possible. They are not collecting your mistakes.
  • Mistakes are information. A wrong note in front of a teacher is not a disaster. It shows exactly where the work is.
  • Your first attempt will be unrepresentative. Nerves change things. Do not judge your playing, or your talent, by how you sounded the first time under pressure.

Why adults struggle more than children

Children often play for others without much fear because they have not yet learned to be embarrassed. Adults arrive with a lifetime of being good at other things. You may run a department, raise a family, or manage a household, and yet a four-bar melody makes your hands shake. That mismatch is uncomfortable, and it makes many self-taught adults avoid playing for anyone at all. I have spent my working life teaching adults who were sure it was too late, and I can tell you that the fear is almost never about the playing. It is about being seen as a beginner. And being seen as a beginner is simply honest, and it is where everyone who ever played began.

A gentle way to start

You do not need to book a recital hall. Build up slowly:

  • Record yourself on your phone, and listen back the next day. This is playing for a listener who is entirely on your side: you.
  • Play one short piece for a family member or friend, and tell them in advance that you are practicing being heard, not asking for criticism.
  • Play the same piece twice in a row for your listener. The second time is nearly always better, because the first has cleared away the surprise.
  • Send a recording to a teacher whose job is to listen.

Each step is small, and each one makes the next easier.

What I took from that room

I came away from playing for Tatiana Nikolayeva understanding that the greatest musicians are, above all, great listeners. They listen so completely that you feel it. If you can learn to play for people the way she listened, with full attention and no hurry to judge, the fear of being heard begins to loosen. Your job is to put the music in front of someone. Their job is to hear it.
