Prokofiev's Music for Children, Op. 65, for Adults

Prokofiev's Music for Children, Op. 65 is serious, witty music in small forms, and several of its pieces are well within reach of an adult who is learning alone.
By Dr. Maria Pisarenko, DMA  ·  January 12, 2027

Taught by Dr. Maria Pisarenko, DMA — the complete beginner course is free: Start at Lesson 1 →

When an adult asks me for music that is not too hard but still worth the effort, I often reach for Prokofiev’s Music for Children, Op. 65. The title puts people off. It should not. These twelve short pieces were written for young players, but they were written by one of the great pianist-composers of the twentieth century, and nothing in the way they are made is childish.

I have given a lecture-recital on Soviet-era programmatic piano music for children, and Prokofiev’s cycle belongs squarely to that tradition. What makes it remarkable is how much character he fits into a page or two. Each piece has a title, a clear picture, and harmony that turns a corner exactly where you do not expect it. That surprise is the whole pleasure.

What is in the collection

The twelve pieces follow the shape of a summer day, from Morning to Evening and finally The Moon Walks over the Meadows. In between you meet a Promenade, a Little Story, a Tarantella, Regrets, a Waltz, the Procession of the Grasshoppers, The Rain and the Rainbow, a game of Tag, and a March. They are not equally easy. Some sit comfortably under the hand; others, like the Tarantella and Tag, ask for speed and a light wrist that take real time to build.

Why this music suits adults

An adult learner usually understands music far better than their fingers can play it. That is the central frustration of starting or returning later in life. Most beginner material solves the finger problem by giving you music with nothing in it. Prokofiev solves it differently: the notes are few, but every one of them means something.

Take Morning. The hands move in wide, calm intervals, often far apart on the keyboard, and the harmony shifts gently from one color to another. Technically it is modest. Musically it asks you to listen to how one chord dissolves into the next, and to hold a quiet, even sound across the whole keyboard. That is exactly the kind of listening an adult can do well, and it trains the ear as much as the hand.

Evening is similar: a long, singing melody over a soft accompaniment, with harmonic turns that sound simple only after you have played them. The Moon Walks over the Meadows asks for the same patience. These three are where I would start most adults.

The pieces to approach with care

The Waltz and the March are good second steps. They introduce clearer rhythm and a steadier pulse, and they show you how much character lives in a sharp accent or a sudden quiet. Regrets is short and slow but emotionally serious; it rewards a careful study of how to shape a phrase that sighs.

Leave the Tarantella, Tag, and the Procession of the Grasshoppers for later. They are delightful, but they depend on quick, relaxed repeated motion, and if you push them too early, tension sets in. There is no prize for getting there fast.

How to practice them alone

Without a teacher, the risk with Prokofiev is that the unusual harmony sounds “wrong” to you at first, and you are not sure whether you misread a note. Here is what I recommend:

  • Listen to a good recording first, once or twice, so your ear knows where the surprises are. Then put it away while you learn the notes.
  • Check every accidental slowly. Prokofiev often changes one note in a chord to shift the color. Read hands separately at first and say the note names out loud when in doubt.
  • Practice slowly, but with the real character. A slow Morning should still sound like morning, not like a reading exercise.
  • Watch the pedal. Many of these pieces need very little. Use your ear: if the harmony blurs, lift sooner.
  • Record yourself after a week and listen back with the score in front of you. You will hear things you cannot hear while playing.

What you gain

A collection like this teaches something that exercises cannot: that a short piece can be complete. You learn to begin with intention, carry a mood through, and end without rushing off the last note. That is the core of playing musically, and it is the same skill you will need later in Mozart, Chopin, or anything else you love.

Prokofiev wrote these pieces to give young players real music from the very start. They do the same for adults. Choose one slow piece, give it two or three weeks, and let it be the first thing you play for yourself that sounds like a composer and not a method book.
