Repeat Signs, D.C., Coda: How to Find Your Way Through a Piece

Da capo, dal segno, the coda sign, first and second endings — these are directions, not music, and they are simpler than they look.
By Dr. Maria Pisarenko, DMA  ·  January 29, 2027

The free course reads from real scores, with every marking explained as it appears: Start the free course →

You can read every note on the page and still get lost, because printed music contains a second layer of instructions that has nothing to do with pitch or rhythm. It tells you the order to play things in.

Music is repetitive by nature, and rather than print the same sixteen bars three times, publishers use a handful of symbols meaning “go back there.” Here is the entire vocabulary.

Repeat bars

A double bar line with two dots before it means: go back and play that section again.

Back to where? To the matching sign with the dots facing the other way. If there is no such sign, back to the very beginning. That is the most common case and the one that catches people out — a single repeat sign late in the page can send you all the way to bar one.

First and second endings

Brackets over the last bars of a repeated section, numbered 1 and 2.

Play through the section including the bar under bracket 1, take the repeat, and the second time through skip the bar under bracket 1 entirely and play the one under bracket 2 instead. Then carry on.

The purpose is ordinary: the section needs one ending to lead back round again, and a different one to move forward.

D.C. and D.S.

D.C. is da capo — “from the head.” Go back to the beginning.

D.S. is dal segno — “from the sign.” Go back to the segno, an ornamented S with a slash and two dots, printed somewhere earlier in the piece. Find it first, before you start playing, or you will be hunting for it at speed.

al Fine added to either means: go back, then play until the word Fine and stop there. The piece ends in the middle of the page, which looks wrong the first time and is correct.

The coda

al Coda means: go back, play forward until you see the coda sign (a circle with a cross through it), then jump to the matching coda sign near the end and play the closing section.

So D.S. al Coda reads: back to the segno, forward to the coda mark, jump to the coda, finish. Three instructions in four characters, which is why it looks like cryptography until you have unpacked it once.

The habit that solves all of this

Trace the route before you play a note. Take a pencil and number the sections in the order you will actually play them — 1, 2, 3 — writing the numbers straight onto the score. Put an arrow where each jump goes.

Thirty seconds, once, and you will never get lost in that piece again. Professional musicians do exactly this, in pencil, on their own copies, and their scores are covered in arrows.

Getting lost in a piece is not a reading failure. It is navigation, and navigators use maps.
