Arpeggios: What They Are and Why They Feel Impossible at First

If arpeggios feel lumpy and uneven no matter how slowly you go, you are almost certainly missing one specific motion.
By Dr. Maria Pisarenko, DMA  ·  January 25, 2027

An arpeggio is a chord played one note at a time instead of all together. The word comes from the Italian for harp, because that is what a harp does with a chord.

Play C, E and G at once and you have a C major chord. Play C, then E, then G, one after the other, and you have arpeggiated it. Same three notes, spread out in time. That is the whole definition, and it explains why arpeggios turn up everywhere in real music: they are how the left hand keeps harmony going underneath a melody without drowning it.

Why they are harder than they look

Three notes, all of them known, in a shape you can already play as a block chord. And yet the arpeggio comes out lumpy, uneven, with a bump in the middle.

The reason is that an arpeggio spans more keyboard than your hand does. A chord sits under a fixed hand. An arpeggio, as soon as it goes past five notes, requires the hand to move along the keyboard while it is playing — and that motion, the thumb passing under, is the whole difficulty.

The movement nobody explains

Here is what most adults do: they keep the hand in one place and stretch the thumb sideways to reach the next note. The stretch creates tension, the tension creates the bump, and the bump never goes away no matter how slowly they play.

What should happen instead: the elbow and forearm travel with the hand. The thumb is not reaching anywhere. The whole arm moves a few inches along the keyboard, carrying the hand into position, and the thumb simply arrives where it needs to be.

And a small thing that makes it possible: as the thumb passes under, let the wrist rise very slightly and the elbow ease out from your side. That clears the road. A locked elbow forces the thumb to do a job it cannot do.

How to practice one

Play it as a block first. C E G together, then move the hand up and play the next C E G together. Get the shape and the jump secure with no melody to think about.

Then break it. Same notes, one at a time, at a speed where you can watch the arm move.

Then listen for the bump. There is always one note that is louder or late — almost always the thumb after it passes under. Play just those two notes, over and over, until they are equal.

Then join it up, slowly, listening for evenness rather than speed.

How long it takes

Less time than you expect, once the arm is involved. The reason arpeggios feel impossible is almost never the notes — it is a stretched thumb and a stationary elbow, and both of those are habits rather than limitations.

A week of five minutes a day, with the arm traveling, and the lumps go out of them.
