The Silence Before the First Chord

The pause before the first note is where the music begins. Dr. Pisarenko describes breathing, hearing the sound first, and a three-step routine that helps adults play with intention.
By Dr. Maria Pisarenko, DMA  ·  October 17, 2026

Taught by Dr. Maria Pisarenko, DMA — the complete beginner course is free: Start at Lesson 1 →

There is a moment in every performance that no one applauds. The pianist is seated, the hands are still, and nothing has happened yet. For me, that silence is the most important part of the music. It is where the piece begins, even though no sound has been made.

What really happens in that moment

When I sit down to play, I am not waiting for courage to arrive. I am doing something specific: I am hearing the first phrase inside. The tempo, the weight, the color of the first chord are already present in my mind before my hand touches the key. By the time the sound comes, it is a reply to something I have already heard.

This is the heart of the approach I learned and now teach from Alexander Alexandrovich Nikolaev’s School of Piano Playing: see it, hear it, then play it. The ear comes first. The hand does not search for the sound; it carries out what the ear has decided. Sound is made by the arm and the whole body of the player, not by a finger jabbing at a key, and you judge it by listening, not by watching your hands.

Why adults rush

When someone is listening, most of us feel an urge to get it over with. The silence feels unbearable, so we start too early, before the breath, before the tempo, before the sound exists in the mind. The result is a first note that is hesitant and a first phrase that is hurried, and the nerves seem to be proven right.

The cure is not to be braver. The cure is to fill the silence with something useful. A pause that is occupied by listening does not feel empty; it feels like preparation.

Breathing as part of the sound

Breath and phrasing are old companions. A singer cannot sing a phrase without a breath, and a pianist who plays as if the piano could breathe for her tends to produce sound that is flat and mechanical. Breathing in the silence before you begin does two things at once. It calms the body, and it sets the length of the phrase. If you breathe in tempo, the tempo will follow.

Many people take a big breath in, which lifts the shoulders. I recommend the opposite: let the breath go out slowly first. The shoulders drop, the arms feel their weight, and the sound that follows tends to be rounder.

Preparing the sound inside

To “hear it before you play it” sounds almost mystical, but it is a practical skill, and you can train it. Try this away from the piano. Think of a melody you know well. Can you hear it in your mind clearly, at a particular speed, with a particular loudness? If so, you already have the ability. The task is to apply it to the piece you are about to play, especially the first phrase.

Many adult learners skip this step because they feel they must prove they can play, and so they start at once. But the first moments set the tone of everything that follows. A piece that begins with a prepared sound tends to continue with one.

A three-step routine for the moment before you play

  • Step one: settle. Sit so that your feet are on the floor and your arms hang freely. Let one slow breath go out, longer than you would normally exhale. Notice your shoulders drop.
  • Step two: hear. Without moving, hear the first phrase inside your head: the speed, the loudness, the shape of the first note. If you cannot hear it clearly, wait. Do not start until you can. Sing it silently if that helps.
  • Step three: send. Let the arm bring the weight to the first key as if answering the sound you imagined. Do not poke at it with a finger. Then keep listening to what comes out.

The whole routine takes only a few seconds, yet it changes the character of a performance. Practice it every single time you sit down, even for a scale, so that it becomes a habit and not a ritual you remember only when you are afraid.

When it goes wrong anyway

Sometimes the first chord comes out wrong. That is not a disaster. Because you heard the sound inside, you also know exactly what you meant, and you can continue toward it. The goal is not an unblemished beginning; it is a beginning with intent. Listeners forgive an imperfect note far more readily than they forgive a performer who has stopped listening.

A small act of respect

The silence before the first chord is also a gift to the people who are listening. It tells them to settle, to put aside their thoughts, to attend. You are not only preparing yourself; you are inviting the room to listen with you. When you play for your family, you can do the same: wait until the talking stops and everyone has arrived, then take your breath and begin.

I have spent my working life teaching adults who were sure it was too late. What they discover, again and again, is that the music they were afraid of is already inside them. The silence is simply the place where they can finally hear it.
