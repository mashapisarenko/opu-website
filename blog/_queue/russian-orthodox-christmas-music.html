Russian Orthodox Christmas and Its Music

An introduction to Russian Orthodox Christmas, its choral and bell traditions, and how they shaped Russian piano sound, with a voicing exercise for adult players.
By Dr. Maria Pisarenko, DMA  ·  January 7, 2027

Taught by Dr. Maria Pisarenko, DMA — the complete beginner course is free: Start at Lesson 1 →

For many Russians, Christmas does not fall on December 25. The Russian Orthodox Church celebrates it on January 7. The date is not a different feast day; it is the same December 25, counted on the older Julian calendar, which the Church continues to use for its festivals. In the twentieth and twenty-first centuries the gap between the two calendars is thirteen days, so the Orthodox Christmas arrives in the first week of January.

The music of that season is quite different from the carols and organ music familiar in the West, and it has shaped the sound of Russian piano music more than many listeners realize.

A tradition of voices

Russian Orthodox worship is sung without instruments. There is no organ and no piano in church. Everything is carried by the human voice: the priest, the deacon and the choir. Over centuries this produced a deep and distinctive choral tradition, from the ancient unison chants, such as znamenny chant, to rich harmonized writing for full choir.

Several of the great Russian composers wrote music for this tradition. Tchaikovsky composed a setting of the Liturgy of St. John Chrysostom, and Rachmaninoff wrote his All-Night Vigil, often called the Vespers, for unaccompanied choir. The Christmas services themselves include a long evening vigil and the Divine Liturgy, sung by the choir with the congregation.

Outside the church, Christmastide was also the season of folk carols, called kolyadki, sung by groups going from house to house. These songs belong to the folk tradition rather than the church, and they carried their own simple, often ancient melodies.

The sound of Russian choirs

A Russian choir has a particular sound. Several features stand out.

  • Very low basses. Russian choral writing often uses bass voices singing exceptionally low notes, sometimes an octave below the usual bass line. The result is a deep foundation under the harmony.
  • Sustained, blended chords. Without instruments, the choir must hold long chords and pass from one to the next smoothly. Every voice must listen to every other.
  • Bells. Bells are central to Russian church life, rung in patterns of overlapping strokes. Their ringing, with its deep resonance and clashing overtones, became a sound composers imitated.

How the choir entered the piano

Russian composers grew up hearing these sounds, and they brought them to the keyboard.

Listen to the opening of Rachmaninoff’s Prelude in C-sharp minor, Op. 3 No. 2. The three heavy notes at the start sound like great bells, and the chords that follow ring and overlap as bells do. Listen to “The Great Gate of Kiev,” the final movement of Mussorgsky’s Pictures at an Exhibition. It contains quiet passages written in plain chords, like a hymn sung by a choir, and later the music fills with the ringing of bells.

More generally, Russian piano writing often treats the instrument as a choir. Chords are spaced so that each voice can be heard. The bass is deep and resonant. Melodies are long and vocal. This is one reason the Russian piano school places such weight on voicing and on a singing tone: the ideal sound in the ear is often a choir.

An exercise in choral playing

You can learn a great deal by playing a simple four-part chorale or hymn at the piano as if it were a choir. Any hymn book or collection of chorales will do.

  • Sing each voice. Before you play, sing or hum the top line, then the bass. Notice that each is a melody of its own.
  • Play the outer voices only. Play the soprano in the right hand and the bass in the left. Make both sing, connecting each note to the next.
  • Add the inner voices softly. Now play all four parts, keeping the two middle voices slightly quieter so the top line and the bass remain clear.
  • Listen for the blend. Hold each chord until you hear it settle. Then move to the next chord as smoothly as a choir would breathe and continue.
  • Try a bell stroke. At the end, play a low octave in the left hand with relaxed arm weight, and let it ring with the pedal. Listen to how long the sound lasts.

This is slow, quiet work, well suited to the dark evenings of early January. It trains your ear to hear several lines at once, and it gives your playing the depth that Russian musicians have always sought: the sound of many voices, carried by one pair of hands.
