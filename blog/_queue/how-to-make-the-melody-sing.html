Two Notes at Once: How to Make the Top One Sing

This is the difference between a chord that sounds like a block and a chord that sounds like music with a melody on top of it.
By Dr. Maria Pisarenko, DMA  ·  November 30, 2026

Harmonic intervals arrive in Lesson 8, in order, free: Open the free course →

For the first months of playing, one hand makes one sound at a time and the questions are about which note and when. Then a piece asks for two notes together in the same hand, and a new question arrives that nobody prepared you for: the two notes come out identical, and the result sounds like a block of sound rather than like music.

The skill that fixes it is called voicing, and it is one of the clearest dividing lines between playing that sounds like a beginner and playing that sounds like a player. It is also not difficult to understand — it is difficult to feel, which is a different problem and a more solvable one.

What your ear is expecting

When several notes sound at once, a listener does not hear them as equals. One of them is the line being followed — almost always the top note — and the others are the harmony underneath it holding it up.

You already know this from listening; it is why you can sing along with a chorus rather than hearing a wash. Your ear expects a melody on top. When you play a chord with every note at the same volume, you are giving your ear something it does not know how to listen to, and that is the exact quality that makes beginner playing sound flat even when every note is right.

The physical move

Two notes in one hand go down at the same moment. The one you want to sing goes down faster.

That is the entire mechanism, and it is worth restating in the way that makes it possible: loud is speed, not force. You are not pressing harder with one finger. You are leaning the weight of the arm slightly toward that side of the hand, so that finger arrives with more speed behind it while the other one goes down gently.

Practically, for a right-hand melody on top: let the hand lean a little toward the little finger, and let the thumb be soft. Most adults do exactly the reverse without knowing it, because the thumb is heavy and strong and takes over by default. A lot of chord playing sounds bottom-heavy for precisely that reason.

How to practice it

Start with two notes, not a chord of four. Take any two notes your hand can hold comfortably — a third apart is ideal — and play them together twenty times, trying to make the top one clearly louder. Then twenty more making the bottom one louder. The second version is harder and it is what teaches you the control.

Exaggerate until you can hear it, then pull it back. Adults almost always undershoot: they think they are making a large difference and the recording shows almost none. Go further than feels right. You can always calm it down once the hand knows the movement.

Play the melody line alone, then add the rest underneath. Play just the top notes of the passage as a tune, so your ear knows what it is listening for. Then put the harmony back in, quietly, keeping the tune exactly as it sounded alone. If the tune changed when you added the other notes, the other notes are too loud.

Hold the chord down and listen to it die. A slow, silent piece of practice: play the chord, keep it held, and listen to which note lasts longest in the room. That is the one that was loudest. It is a more honest test than your impression while playing.

Why the interval itself matters

Two notes together do not only carry a melody — they carry a character, and it comes from the distance between them.

Neighbors played together sound tense and unsettled; that clash is what film music uses when something is about to go wrong. Notes further apart sound open and calm. Beginners often meet this first as "that sounds wrong", and it is not wrong — it is a color with a purpose, and recognizing it is the beginning of hearing harmony rather than only notes.

It is worth sitting at the instrument for two minutes and playing pairs of notes just to listen to the difference. That is theory learned the way it is actually useful: through the ear first, with the name attached afterward.

Where this shows up next

Once two notes are under control, the same skill scales up. A three-note chord in the right hand with the tune on top is the same lean. Hands together, the entire left hand becomes the accompaniment and needs to sit under the entire right hand — the same principle applied at a larger size.

It is one of the few skills in early playing that keeps paying for years, which is why it is worth spending real practice time on rather than hoping it arrives on its own. It does not arrive on its own.

The part you cannot hear

Balance inside a single hand is almost impossible to judge while playing. You know which note is the melody, so you hear it as prominent whether or not it actually is — and the recording routinely disagrees with the player.

So record one minute of a passage with chords, with the phone far enough back to catch the room rather than the strings, and send it. The written feedback is free, and voicing is one of the things a second pair of ears identifies fastest — and one of the quickest to change once you know which way your hand is leaning.
