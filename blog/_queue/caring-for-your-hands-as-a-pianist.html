Caring for Your Hands as a Pianist

Good piano habits protect your hands: a gentle warm-up, regular breaks, a free arm and a sound posture, and a doctor's visit whenever playing causes pain.
By Dr. Maria Pisarenko, DMA  ·  January 17, 2027

Taught by Dr. Maria Pisarenko, DMA — the complete beginner course is free: Start at Lesson 1 →

Adults who come back to the piano are usually careful people. They have jobs, responsibilities, and a healthy respect for their own bodies. And yet, once they sit down to practice alone, many of them work in exactly the way that tires the hands: long stretches without a break, the same difficult passage repeated thirty times, shoulders slowly creeping up toward the ears.

I want to say one thing clearly before anything else. I am a pianist and a teacher, not a doctor. If playing causes you pain, numbness, tingling, or weakness, stop and see a doctor. Do not try to practice through it, and do not rely on an article, including this one, to diagnose it. What follows is about habits that help healthy hands stay comfortable at the piano.

Begin gently

You do not need an elaborate warm-up routine. You need a gradual start. Cold hands and a cold mind tend to grab the keys. I suggest the first five minutes of any session be easy and slow.

  • Wash your hands in warm water or simply rub them together if they feel cold.
  • Play something slow and familiar: a scale at a comfortable tempo, a few broken chords, or a quiet piece you already know.
  • Listen for an even, relaxed sound rather than speed or power.

The aim is not to stretch or strengthen anything. It is to let the arms and fingers find the keyboard calmly before you ask them for real work.

Take real breaks

Concentration and muscles both tire, and they tire before you notice. A simple rule works well: after twenty or twenty-five minutes, stand up. Walk around the room, let your arms hang, look at something far away. Then come back.

Breaks also improve practice. Many adults find that a passage which refused to work before a pause suddenly goes better after it. That is not luck. A rested mind and a rested hand learn more efficiently than tired ones.

Vary the work as well. If you have spent ten minutes on a fast passage, move to something slow and lyrical. Repeating one demanding motion for a long time is far harder on the hands than the same total time spread across different kinds of playing.

Notice tension early

Tension at the piano rarely announces itself. It builds quietly: a thumb that presses after the key is already down, a wrist that locks during a difficult jump, a jaw that clenches when you concentrate. Learning alone, you need to become your own observer.

  • Check your shoulders every few minutes. If they have risen, let them drop.
  • Once a note is down, let go. The key does not need to be pushed into the bed of the keyboard. Release the extra pressure as soon as the sound begins.
  • Breathe. Many people hold their breath in hard passages. Breathing with the phrase helps the whole body stay free.
  • Slow down when a passage makes you tense. Tension usually means you are asking for more speed than the hand is ready for.

Sit well

Posture is where good hand habits begin. A poor seat makes the arms and hands do work that should be shared by the whole body.

  • Height: your forearms should be roughly level with the keys, or slightly above, so the wrist is neither sagging nor pointed up. An adjustable bench is worth the investment.
  • Distance: sit far enough back that your elbows are a little in front of your body and can move freely. Too close, and the arms get cramped.
  • Balance: sit on the front half of the bench with both feet on the floor, so you can lean slightly toward the keyboard from the hips.
  • Back: upright but not rigid. Think of being tall and loose at the same time.

Check this in a mirror or with a quick photo from the side. Most adults are surprised by what they see.

Plan sensible practice

For a returning adult, two shorter sessions in a day are often better than one long one. Increase your practice time gradually, the way you would with any new physical activity. If you have a long session coming, perhaps before playing for friends, build up to it over several days rather than jumping there at once.

And listen to your body honestly. Ordinary tiredness after good work goes away with rest. Pain does not belong to piano playing. If it appears, or if discomfort keeps coming back, that is the moment to stop and see a doctor.

A small routine to try this week

Start each session with five easy minutes. Set a timer for twenty-five minutes and stand up when it rings. Check your shoulders each time you turn a page. Look at your seat from the side once. These are small changes, but they are the habits that let a pianist keep playing for decades.
