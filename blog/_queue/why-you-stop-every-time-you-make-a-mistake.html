Why You Stop Every Time You Make a Mistake (and How to Stop Stopping)

Nobody decides to do this. It happens by itself, it feels responsible, and it quietly builds a piece that has a perfect opening and no ending.
By Dr. Maria Pisarenko, DMA  ·  December 16, 2026

Watch an adult practicing alone and you will see the same thing over and over. A wrong note in bar three, a small flinch, hands lift, and back to the beginning. Wrong note in bar five this time. Back to the beginning. Half an hour later the first two bars are immaculate and the end of the piece has been played twice.

Nobody decides to practice this way. It arrives on its own, it feels like conscientiousness, and it is doing two kinds of damage at once.

What it actually teaches

Your hands learn what you do, not what you intend. If every attempt runs beginning → mistake → stop, then what is being rehearsed, dozens of times a week, is the stopping. The flinch becomes part of the piece. Later, in front of another person, a small slip triggers a full halt — not because you panicked but because that is the sequence you practiced.

Meanwhile the parts of the piece that need the most work are getting the least of it, because you only ever reach them exhausted, and often not at all.

The habit is a good instinct pointed the wrong way

The instinct underneath is correct: that was wrong, it should not be left wrong. Quite right. The error is in what happens next. Going back to the top does not fix the wrong place. It only reaches it again, in the same condition, with the same result.

So keep the instinct and change the destination.

The fix: mark it and keep going

Give yourself two clearly separate activities, and never mix them.

Playing through. You start, and you do not stop for anything. A wrong note is not an event. Keep the beat, keep going, let it be imperfect. If you lose your place entirely, find the next downbeat you can and rejoin there. The single rule is that the music does not stop.

Working. Afterwards — and only afterwards — take the two or three places that went wrong, and work on those places alone, small and slow, the way practice is supposed to go. Two bars, not two pages.

Keep a pencil on the music desk. When something goes wrong in a play-through, mark it with a small cross and carry on. The cross is what lets you keep going without feeling that you are ignoring the fault: you are not ignoring it, you are filing it. The crosses are your practice list for the next fifteen minutes.

It will feel wrong for about a week

Playing past a mistake feels sloppy, especially to people who are careful about their work in every other part of their lives. Expect that. What you are actually practicing is something musicians have to have: the ability to keep the piece alive while something is going wrong inside it. Every professional does this constantly. Audiences do not hear most of it precisely because nobody stopped.

After a week you will notice something else. The middle of the piece starts to improve — because for the first time it is being played as often as the beginning.

One session, both halves

A good half hour looks like this. Five minutes: play the piece through, no stopping, pencil marks where it slipped. Twenty minutes: work on the marked places, slowly, isolated. Five minutes: play it through again, no stopping, and let it be whatever it is.

Finish with the play-through, not with the repairs. The last thing you did in a session is the thing that stays with you — and you want that to be the experience of getting to the end.
