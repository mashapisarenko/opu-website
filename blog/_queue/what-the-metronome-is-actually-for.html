What a Metronome Is Actually For (It Is Not Making You Play Faster)

A metronome does not make you play faster. It shows you where your playing is uneven — which is uncomfortable, and is exactly the information you need.
By Dr. Maria Pisarenko, DMA  ·  December 11, 2026

Counting comes before the metronome. It is taught from Lesson 3 of the free course: Start the free course →

Almost every adult beginner buys a metronome, uses it for a week, finds it unpleasant, and quietly stops. The reason is that they were sold the wrong idea of what it does.

A metronome is not a speed trainer and it is not a discipline device. It is a measuring instrument. It tells you, with no tact whatsoever, where your playing is uneven. That is all it does, and that is worth a great deal — but only if you use it on something you can already play.

Count first. The metronome comes later.

If you cannot yet count a passage out loud in time, a metronome will not help you. It will simply be a second thing to fail at.

Counting out loud is the primary skill: you say the beat, so the beat comes from you. A metronome does the opposite — the beat comes from outside and your job is to agree with it. That is a harder task and it belongs after the first one, not instead of it. Learn the counting, and bring the metronome in when the counting is already steady.

What it is actually showing you

Turn it on over a passage you know, and listen for the places where you and the click part company. They are never random. In beginner playing they are almost always in the same three places:

Long notes get cut short. A half note is held for something like a beat and a half, because holding still feels like doing nothing and the hand is impatient to continue.

Hard bits get slower and easy bits get faster. This one is universal. Your tempo is a map of your confidence, and the metronome draws that map for you in about eight bars.

The beat after a mistake disappears. Something goes wrong, you flinch, and half a beat vanishes while you recover. On your own you never notice it. Against a click it is obvious.

None of that is a character flaw. It is information, and you cannot fix a wobble you cannot hear.

The two common misuses

The first is using it to learn a piece. If the notes are not secure, the click just makes you tense and you learn the piece badly at a speed you cannot control. Learn the notes first, slowly and freely. The metronome checks work; it does not do work.

The second is chasing the number. Somebody sets it faster every day and treats the reading as a score. Speed obtained that way is fragile — it falls apart the moment you are nervous, because it was never even, it was only fast. If a passage is not clean at a given tempo, going faster does not fix it, it hides it.

How to actually use it

Set it slower than your comfortable speed. Slower — you want room to be accurate. Play the passage four times. If all four are clean and with the click, move it up by a small step, not a large one, and do it again. The moment a repetition is not clean, go back down.

Ten minutes of that is plenty. It is concentrated work and there is no advantage in doing it until you are irritated.

And turn it off to play. A piece performed with a click is not a piece performed. Music breathes — it leans forward slightly here, gives a little there, waits at a fermata. That flexibility is only musical when it is deliberate, and the way to make it deliberate is to know exactly what the steady version sounds like. Which is the real reason to own a metronome.

The phone is fine

You do not need a wooden pyramid on top of the piano. A free app does the same job. What matters is not the device but the habit: use it on something you can play, use it to find the uneven place, and turn it off before you make music.
