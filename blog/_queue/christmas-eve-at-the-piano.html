Christmas Eve at the Piano: Playing for Your Family

Playing for family on Christmas Eve is a gift, not a test; here is how to choose music within reach, set up the room and play calmly through the nerves.
By Dr. Maria Pisarenko, DMA  ·  December 24, 2026

Taught by Dr. Maria Pisarenko, DMA — the complete beginner course is free: Start at Lesson 1 →

There is something special about playing the piano on Christmas Eve. The house is full, the lights are low, and someone says, “Will you play something?” For an adult who has been learning alone, this can be the moment you have quietly been working toward, and also the moment you most dread. With a little preparation, it can be a gift for everyone, including you.

Choose something within reach

The single most important decision is what to play, and the right answer is almost always something easier than you think. Performing makes everything harder. Hands that are steady at home can tremble in front of family. A piece you play well nine times out of ten in private may come out five times out of ten with people watching.

So choose music you can already play comfortably, not music you are still learning. A good test: can you play it from beginning to end, at a slightly slower tempo, without stopping, on a tired evening? If yes, it is ready.

  • A simple carol. Something everyone knows, like “Silent Night” or “O Come, All Ye Faithful,” in a version you play easily. Familiar tunes invite people to listen warmly, or even sing.
  • A short piece you love. One page is plenty. Nobody needs a recital.
  • Melody with simple chords. If a full arrangement is too much, play the melody in the right hand with single bass notes or simple chords in the left. Played with care, this sounds lovely.

Prepare in the days before

In the week before, play your chosen piece once a day as a performance: start, do not stop, finish. If you make a mistake, keep going. This is the habit that will save you on the night.

It also helps to play it once for one person in advance, or record it on your phone. The nervous feeling arrives the first time someone listens. Better to meet it before Christmas Eve than during.

Set up the room

A few practical things make a big difference.

  • Light. Holiday lighting is beautiful but often dim. Make sure you can see your music clearly; a small lamp near the piano is worth it.
  • The bench. Check its height before you start. Relatives and guests may have moved it.
  • The music. Put your score on the stand in advance, open to the right page, with any page turns solved. If a turn is awkward, a photocopy laid out side by side avoids it.
  • Clear the piano lid. Decorations and cards on the piano can buzz when certain notes sound.
  • Warm hands. Cold fingers feel clumsy. Wash your hands in warm water a few minutes before you play.

When you are nervous

Nerves are normal. Every performer feels them. The goal is not to get rid of them but to play well alongside them.

  • Take your time before you begin. Sit, adjust the bench, put your hands in your lap and breathe out slowly. There is no rush.
  • Hear the first bar in your head. Before your hands move, imagine the opening at the right speed. Nerves tend to make us start too fast.
  • Choose a slightly slower tempo. What feels slow to you will sound calm and confident to listeners.
  • Keep going. If a note goes wrong, continue. Your family is listening to the music and to you, not checking notes against a score.
  • Think of one listener. Instead of the whole room, play for one person you love. It makes the room feel smaller and warmer.

What matters on the night

Your family will not remember whether every note was right. They will remember that you sat down and played for them, and how the room felt while you did. A simple carol, played warmly and calmly, can be the most moving moment of the evening. Let the music be a gift rather than a test.

This week at the piano

Choose your Christmas Eve piece today, something easy enough to play on a tired evening. Each day this week, play it once from beginning to end without stopping, at a calm tempo, and record it once before the weekend. Then, on Christmas Eve, sit down, breathe out, and give it to them.
