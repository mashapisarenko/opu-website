Accompanying a Choir or Singer: What the Pianist Does

Accompanying singers asks a pianist for steady rhythm, quick reading, and a feel for breath, and an adult learning alone can start practicing all three today.
By Dr. Maria Pisarenko, DMA  ·  January 24, 2027

Taught by Dr. Maria Pisarenko, DMA — the complete beginner course is free: Start at Lesson 1 →

For many adults, accompanying is the most practical musical goal there is. A church or community choir needs a pianist. A friend sings and would love someone to play for them. Holiday songs at a family gathering sound better with a piano. Accompanying is a way to make music with other people, and it is a skill that can be learned.

I have performed as a soloist with choir and with orchestra, and I have great respect for the pianists who sit beside singers week after week. Their work looks modest from the audience. In fact it asks for a particular set of skills, different in some ways from solo playing.

What an accompanist really does

An accompanist provides three things: a harmonic foundation, a steady pulse, and support for the singers’ breath and pitch. In rehearsal, the pianist may also play the individual vocal parts so each section can learn its notes, give starting pitches, and follow the director’s changes of tempo without hesitation.

The word “accompanist” can sound secondary. It is not. A choir that is supported well sings with more confidence. A choir with an unsteady pianist loses its rhythm and its pitch together.

Steadiness above everything

The most important quality in an accompanist is a reliable pulse. A soloist can stop and fix a wrong note. An accompanist cannot. If you hesitate, thirty singers hesitate with you. The professional rule is simple: never stop. If you miss a note, keep the beat. If a passage is too hard, simplify it on the spot and keep going.

This is a real shift for adults who have learned alone, because most of us practice by stopping and correcting. Accompanying requires the opposite habit, practiced deliberately.

Breathing with singers

Singers phrase with their breath. A good accompanist hears and feels where the breath falls and leaves room for it. Before a phrase begins, the pianist gives a clear, unhurried introduction or chord so the singers know exactly when to come in. At the end of a phrase, the pianist does not rush into the next bar if the singers need a moment to breathe.

The best way to learn this is to sing yourself. When you sing a melody, you discover where breath is natural and where a phrase must not be broken.

Balance and support

The piano can easily cover voices, especially when singers are few or when they sing softly. Accompanists learn to play lighter than a solo pianist would, to keep the left hand clear rather than heavy, and to bring out the vocal line when singers need help with pitch, then step back when they are confident.

Reading and simplifying

Choir and song accompaniments are often written for a fluent pianist. A useful skill is knowing what can be left out. The bass line and the harmony matter most. Inner notes and decorative figures can be thinned when necessary. Learning to reduce a texture without losing its shape is a valuable musical skill in its own right.

What you can practice alone

You can develop most of these skills at home, without a choir.

  • Play through without stopping. Choose a simple hymn or folk song arrangement and play it from beginning to end with a metronome, never stopping for mistakes.
  • Sing while you play. Play the accompaniment and sing the melody. It feels awkward at first, but it teaches you where the breath falls and how loud the piano should be.
  • Play from a hymnal. Four-part hymns are excellent reading practice, and they are the core of much choir accompaniment.
  • Practice reduced versions. Play only the bass line and the melody, then add the harmony gradually. This trains you to simplify under pressure.
  • Accompany recordings. Play along with a recording of a singer performing a simple song, matching their breathing and timing.
  • Play introductions with intention. Practice the first bars of a song as if singers were waiting for you: steady, clear, and in exactly the tempo you want.

Finding a place to start

When you feel ready, offer to play for one singer first, perhaps a friend or family member, with one simple song you have prepared well. A small community or church group is a natural next step, and many are glad of a willing pianist even at a modest level.

This week, take one simple song you know, and play it through three times without stopping while singing the melody. That small exercise is where every accompanist begins.
