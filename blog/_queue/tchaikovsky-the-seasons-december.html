Tchaikovsky's December: A Winter Waltz for Adult Pianists

Tchaikovsky's December is a gentle Christmas waltz written for home music-making; here is what makes it charming and how an adult learning alone can approach it.
By Dr. Maria Pisarenko, DMA  ·  December 8, 2026

Taught by Dr. Maria Pisarenko, DMA — the complete beginner course is free: Start at Lesson 1 →

The last page of Tchaikovsky’s The Seasons belongs to winter. It is called “December: Christmas,” and it is a waltz. Not a carol, not a hymn, not a festive march with bells. A waltz, quiet and graceful, the kind of music you might hear drifting from another room on a winter evening. For an adult learning piano alone, it is one of the best pieces to know, whether you play it soon or simply listen with new ears.

Twelve months in music

The Seasons, Op. 37a, is a set of twelve character pieces, one for each month. Tchaikovsky wrote them for a monthly music magazine in St. Petersburg, so each piece reached readers in its own month, a little musical calendar page. Each one carries a short epigraph from a Russian poem. December’s verse comes from the poet Vasily Zhukovsky and describes young women on Christmas Eve telling their fortunes, a playful winter custom.

That is the first thing to understand about this music. These pieces were written for home use, for people who played the piano in their living rooms. They are not concert showpieces. They are intimate, made to be shared with a few people close by.

What makes December charming

The piece is in A-flat major and in three-four time, marked as a waltz. Listen to a recording and notice three things.

  • The lilt. A good waltz is not three equal beats. The first beat is grounded, the second and third are lighter, almost floating. Tchaikovsky’s left hand gives you that bass note and two soft chords, and the melody leans gently on top.
  • The turns of phrase. The melody rises and sighs, then answers itself. It sounds like conversation, not a speech. Many phrases end with a small graceful falling figure, like a dancer settling after a turn.
  • The return. After a contrasting middle passage, the opening tune comes back, and it feels like coming back indoors from the cold.

The charm is in restraint. Nothing is loud for long. Nothing shows off. A pianist who plays it well sounds as if they are remembering something rather than performing it.

Is it within reach?

I will be honest. In its original form, “December” is an intermediate piece. It asks for comfortable reading in four flats, a steady waltz bass that leaps across the keyboard, ornamental notes in the right hand, and careful pedaling. If you are in your first year at the piano, it is a goal rather than this month’s project.

But that does not mean you must wait to enjoy it at the keyboard. There are simplified arrangements for early players, and many of them keep the melody and the waltz feeling while reducing the left hand to single notes. A good simplified version is a door, not a lesser version. You learn the shape of the music now, and when you meet the original later, it will feel like greeting an old friend.

Simple ways to approach music like this

Whether you work from an arrangement or simply want to understand the style, here is what I suggest.

  • Waltz the left hand alone. Play any simple pattern in three: a low note on beat one, a lighter chord or two notes on beats two and three. Say “down, up, up” out loud. The goal is that beat one feels like stepping onto the floor and the others feel like lifting off it.
  • Sing the melody before you play it. Hum the tune from a recording. Where do you breathe? Those breaths are where the phrases end, and your hand should lift there too.
  • Keep everything soft. Practice the whole piece, or your arrangement, at a quiet level. This music lives between soft and medium. If you can play softly and still make the melody heard above the accompaniment, you have learned the most important skill the piece teaches.
  • Do not rush the endings. Each phrase ending deserves a tiny bit of time. Not a stop, just a breath. That is where the tenderness lives.

Listen like a pianist

Find two different recordings of “December” and listen to each one twice. The first time, simply enjoy it. The second time, listen for one thing only: how the pianist plays the left hand. Does the bass ring? Are the chords on beats two and three light, or heavy? Great pianists make very different choices, and all of them still sound like a waltz. Musical taste is not one correct answer; it is a set of careful decisions.

This week at the piano

Choose any simple piece you already know in three-four time, or make up a left-hand waltz pattern on a C major chord. Play it for five minutes a day with one goal: beat one grounded, beats two and three light. Then listen to Tchaikovsky’s “December” once before bed. By the end of the week, you will hear the waltz in it differently, because you will have felt it in your own hands.
