Best 88-Key Weighted Keyboard for Beginners (2026): A Teacher's Picks

If you want 88 weighted keys to learn on, these are the ones I would look at and the key facts for each.

An 88-key weighted keyboard is the sensible starting point for an adult who wants to learn piano. The ones I would look at are the Yamaha P-145BT, Roland FP-10 and Yamaha P-45, with the Casio CDP-S360 and Donner DDP-300 as alternatives. All are described by their listings as weighted or hammer-action.

Quick picks

  • Best overall: Yamaha P-145BT — 88 keys on a GHC keyboard
  • Best key feel: Roland FP-10 — PHA-4 Standard keyboard with escapement
  • Best budget alternative: Donner DDP-300 — 88 graded hammer-action keys with stated polyphony

How I choose

I check that the listing says weighted, graded or hammer action, because the words semi-weighted and weighted are easy to confuse. I have not played every instrument or used every device in this guide, and I do not say that I have. What I give you is what I look for as a concert pianist who teaches adults, checked against what each Amazon listing and manufacturer page actually states.

The picks

Yamaha P-145BT

Who it is for: An adult beginner who plays along with apps and wants the right keys without paying for extras.

What the listing says: Yamaha lists 88 keys on a GHC keyboard with matte black keytops, four touch settings (hard, medium, soft, fixed), 64-note polyphony, 10 voices, Bluetooth audio, one headphone jack, a footswitch in the box and a weight of 11.1 kg.

What I look for: A graded keyboard with a surface that does not slip, and a touch setting I can leave on medium and forget. For a first piano I would rather see a good action and few voices than the reverse.

The one I recommend most often to adults.

Pros
  • Graded-hammer-style keyboard in a light body
  • Bluetooth audio for playing along with a recording
Cons
  • 64-note polyphony is the lowest in this class
  • Only 10 voices

Buy it if you want one right instrument for the first few years and plan to spend the savings on a pedal and a bench. Skip it if you want two headphone jacks or a bigger sound for pedal-heavy repertoire.

Roland FP-10

Who it is for: An adult beginner in a small room who wants a proper piano action and nothing extra.

What the listing says: Roland lists 88 keys on its PHA-4 Standard keyboard with escapement and ivory-feel white keys, 96-voice polyphony, two 12 cm speakers, one headphone jack, a pedal switch in the box and a weight of 12.6 kg without the music rest.

What I look for: Whether the keys feel graded (heavier in the bass, lighter in the treble), whether quiet notes sound at the touch you meant, and whether a repeated note answers quickly. Escapement, the small notch you feel as the key lets go of the hammer, is the first thing I check on a beginner piano because it teaches control of soft playing.

A compact option with the best-known Roland action.

Pros
  • Real graded action in a compact, light body
  • Simple controls that do not distract a beginner
Cons
  • 96-voice polyphony runs out sooner with long pedaled passages
  • One headphone jack, so a teacher cannot listen alongside you

Buy it if you want the best key feel for a modest budget and a small footprint. Skip it if you already know you will play pedal-heavy Romantic pieces, or you want a second headphone jack.

Yamaha P-45

Who it is for: A first-year beginner who needs weighted keys at the lowest sensible entry point.

What the listing says: The listing says 88 weighted keys, with a music rest and a sustain pedal.

What I look for: Weighted keys are the point. I would play a few slow five-finger patterns and listen for even volume from note to note.

A lower-cost way into a Yamaha weighted action.

Pros
  • Weighted keys at an entry level
  • Music rest and sustain pedal listed with it
Cons
  • Plainer sound engine than the models above it
  • The listing gives few specifications, so check the page before you buy

Buy it if you want weighted keys and your budget is tight. Skip it if you want a richer sound or a quick path to Bluetooth and USB audio.

Casio CDP-S360

Who it is for: A budget-minded beginner who wants scaled hammer-action keys in a light body.

What the listing says: The listing says 88 full-size scaled hammer-action keys, in a slim body of about 24 pounds.

What I look for: I would check that the bass keys feel heavier than the treble keys, the way a real piano does, and that fast repeated notes sound clean.

A scaled hammer action in a slim body.

Pros
  • Scaled hammer-action keys
  • Slim body
Cons
  • Fewer specifications on the listing than the Yamaha or Roland pages
  • Plainer styling

Buy it if you want a graded action and a slim body. Skip it if you want the detail of a Roland or Yamaha specification page before you buy.

Donner DDP-300

Who it is for: A careful budget with weighted keys and a stated polyphony.

What the listing says: The listing says 88 graded hammer-action weighted keys with 10 grand piano timbres and 128-note polyphony.

What I look for: Graded hammer action is the right description; I would check how the bass keys feel against the treble.

A budget alternative that states its polyphony clearly.

Pros
  • Graded hammer action, 128-note polyphony
  • Specifications stated clearly on the listing
Cons
  • Less established than Yamaha or Roland
  • Resale and service are harder to judge

Buy it if you want graded keys at a modest budget. Skip it if you want the reassurance of a major piano maker.

Questions adults ask

What does weighted mean on a keyboard?

Weighted keys resist your finger the way a piano's hammer action does, so you learn to control volume with touch.

Are all 88-key keyboards weighted?

No. Some are semi-weighted or unweighted. Read the listing for the words weighted, graded or hammer action.

Is 88 keys necessary?

For most piano music, yes. It costs little more than 76 and avoids limits later.
