Best Keyboard for Beginners: A Piano Teacher's Pick

A good beginner keyboard is touch-sensitive, full-size and quiet when you need it. Here is how to choose.
By Dr. Maria Pisarenko, DMA  ·  November 14, 2026

Forty-one lessons in order, free, at your own pace: Start the free course →

The best keyboard for beginners is one with touch-sensitive keys that fits your space. My pick is the Casio CT-S1, a compact 61-key keyboard. But if you can afford it, a weighted 88-key digital piano is a better first instrument for learning piano.

What is the best keyboard for a beginner?

It depends on what the keyboard is for. If you want to learn piano, the first choice is a weighted digital piano; read digital piano vs keyboard for the reason. If space, travel or budget tier rules that out, a touch-sensitive portable keyboard is a reasonable start, and the list below is what I would choose from.

What features matter in a beginner keyboard?

Touch sensitivity. It is what lets soft and loud sound different, the first musical habit I teach. Without it every note sounds the same however hard the finger works. Full-size keys. They teach the hand how far a real keyboard stretches. A headphone jack. Daily practice depends on it. A music rest and a pedal input. Without a pedal input you cannot learn pedal technique. Power: mains, batteries or both.

What matters less: the number of sounds, rhythms, recording gadgets and built-in lessons.

Do I need 61 or 88 keys?

Sixty-one is five octaves and is enough to begin; 88 is the full range and what a real piano has. See 61 vs 76 vs 88 keys for how soon you will hit the limit. If you start on 61, plan on moving to 88 weighted keys within the first year.

Is a keyboard good enough for the free course?

Yes, for the early lessons. Many students begin the free 41-lesson course on whatever they have, and a touch-sensitive keyboard is a perfectly reasonable place to start finding notes, counting and reading. What the keyboard cannot teach is the weight of a real key, so as the pieces grow and your hands strengthen, you will feel the limit. That is the signal to move up to a weighted digital piano, and the sooner you do, the less you will have to unlearn.

What accessories does a keyboard need?

A stand that is stable and the right height, such as the OnStage KT7800+; a bench you can adjust (how to sit); a sustain pedal if the keyboard has an input (the guide); and wired headphones (my picks). A keyboard on a dining table with a dining chair is the most common bad setup I see described.

Should I buy a keyboard bundle?

Bundles are tempting because they include a stand, a bench, headphones and a pedal in one box. Read the contents carefully: the pieces are often the cheapest of their kind, and the bench in particular may not adjust. The Alesis Recital Play 88-key keyboard bundle is in my shop list; check the maker's page for what it includes and its key action before buying. I would rather you chose the keyboard well and added the bench and headphones separately.

Can I record a keyboard to send for feedback?

Yes. Record with a phone or a small recorder like the Zoom H1n (90-degree XY stereo microphones, per Zoom) and send the clip on the feedback page. A clear recording is all I need to hear your touch and rhythm.

Questions adults ask

What is the best keyboard for a beginner?

A touch-sensitive portable keyboard with full-size keys, such as the Casio CT-S1, if space or budget requires it. A weighted 88-key digital piano is better for learning piano.

Is a 61-key keyboard good for learning piano?

It is good for starting out. You will meet its limit within the first year, so plan to move to a weighted 88-key piano.

Do I need touch-sensitive keys?

Yes. Touch sensitivity lets soft and loud sound different, which is the first musical habit a pianist builds.

Can I learn piano from a keyboard's built-in lessons?

They help you find notes but cannot see your wrist or hear your touch. A teacher or the free course is a better guide.

Should I buy a MIDI keyboard to learn piano?

A MIDI controller makes no sound by itself and needs software. It suits students who already use apps, not as a first instrument.
