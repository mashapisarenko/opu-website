What Happens When You Press a Piano Key

Pressing a piano key sets off a chain of levers, a thrown hammer and a lifting damper; knowing how it works explains why touch, not force, makes the sound.
By Dr. Maria Pisarenko, DMA  ·  December 17, 2026

Taught by Dr. Maria Pisarenko, DMA — the complete beginner course is free: Start at Lesson 1 →

When you press a piano key, it feels like a single simple action. In fact you have set off a small chain of levers, springs, felt and wood, all happening in a fraction of a second. You do not need to know the engineering to play well. But understanding a little of what happens inside the instrument explains why touch matters so much, and why two people can play the same note on the same piano and make two different sounds.

The key is a lever

Each key is a long wooden lever resting on a balance point, like a seesaw. When you press the front end down, the back end rises inside the piano. That rising end lifts a set of parts called the action. A grand piano has one complete action mechanism for every key, eighty-eight in all.

The hammer is thrown, not pushed

Here is the most important thing to understand. The hammer, a small wooden head covered in dense felt, is not pressed against the string. It is thrown at it.

As the key goes down, a part called the jack pushes the hammer upward. Just before the hammer reaches the string, the jack slips out from under it. This release is called escapement. For the last tiny distance, the hammer travels freely, strikes the string and immediately bounces away. If it stayed pressed against the string, it would stop the vibration and you would hear a dull thud instead of a tone.

This means that once the hammer has left the jack, nothing you do with your finger can change that note’s attack. The sound was decided in the moment before escapement, by how fast the hammer was moving.

Speed is the sound

A faster hammer produces a louder sound, and also a brighter one. A slower hammer produces a softer, rounder sound. So when we talk about touch, we are really talking about how you control the speed of the key on its way down.

That is why pressing harder after the key has reached the bottom does nothing for the sound, except tire your hand. The work happens on the way down. It is also why pianists talk about weight and preparation rather than force: a relaxed arm can give the key a controlled speed far more reliably than a stiff finger can.

The dampers

Resting on the strings of most notes is a damper, a block of felt that keeps the string silent. When you press a key, its damper lifts at the same time, so the string can vibrate. When you let the key up, the damper falls back and the sound stops.

This explains two things every learner meets early.

  • Legato depends on release. To connect two notes smoothly, the second key must go down just as the first key comes up. Release too early and there is a gap of silence. Release too late and the notes overlap.
  • The sustain pedal lifts all the dampers. With the right pedal down, every string is free to ring, which is why sound continues after you let go and why notes blend together. Using it well means lifting it in time to let the dampers clear the old harmony.

The very highest strings have no dampers at all. Their sound is short anyway, and leaving them free adds resonance.

Fast repetition

A grand piano action also has a repetition mechanism that catches the hammer as it falls and holds it ready. This lets you play the same note again without the key rising all the way. Upright pianos are built differently, with the action standing vertically, and many do not repeat as quickly. That is not a fault in you if a fast repeated note feels harder on an upright.

What this means for your playing

  • Prepare before you play. Touch the key surface before pressing. Starting from contact gives you control of speed; striking from above often gives an accidental accent.
  • Listen to the release. The end of a note is as much a part of the music as its start. Lift at the moment you mean to.
  • Do not press into the bottom. Once the key is down, let the arm rest lightly. The sound is already made.
  • Know your instrument. A digital piano imitates this mechanism with sensors. Good ones respond to speed, which is why a weighted, touch-sensitive keyboard matters if you practice on one.

This week at the piano

Choose one note in the middle of the keyboard. Rest your finger on the key, then press it at five different speeds, from very slow to quite fast, and listen to how the sound changes in both volume and color. Then practice letting the key rise at the exact moment you choose. Five minutes a day of this is a lesson in touch that no book can give you.
