Chopin's Nocturnes: How to Listen and Where to Start

Chopin's Nocturnes join an operatic melody to a flowing left hand and a flexible rubato, and a few of them are within reach of an adult learning alone.
By Dr. Maria Pisarenko, DMA  ·  January 28, 2027

Taught by Dr. Maria Pisarenko, DMA — the complete beginner course is free: Start at Lesson 1 →

For many adults, Chopin is the reason they wanted to play the piano in the first place. And among all his music, the Nocturnes are perhaps the most loved: slow, intimate pieces with long, singing melodies over a gently moving accompaniment. They sound like night music because they are night music, written for an evening room rather than a concert hall.

Before you try to play them, it helps to know how to listen to them. Once you understand what is happening in a nocturne, you will hear it differently, and you will practice it far more wisely.

Where the nocturne came from

The piano nocturne was not Chopin’s invention. The Irish composer John Field wrote the first pieces with that title, and Chopin knew them. But Chopin took the form much further, with richer harmony, more dramatic contrasts, and a depth of feeling that made the nocturne one of the central genres of Romantic piano music. He wrote about twenty of them across his life, some published after his death.

The melody: bel canto at the piano

Chopin loved opera, particularly the Italian style called bel canto, “beautiful singing.” Its melodies are long and smooth, with ornaments that decorate a line the way a great singer would. The right hand in a nocturne is essentially a soprano.

This is the first thing to listen for. Notice how the melody breathes, where it rises to a peak and falls back. Notice the ornaments: small runs and turns that should sound like a voice gliding, not like a pianist showing off fingers. When you play, ask yourself constantly whether a singer could perform the line the way you are playing it.

The left hand: the orchestra underneath

Under the melody, the left hand usually plays a flowing accompaniment, often wide broken chords that sweep from a low bass note up through the harmony. In a good performance you hardly notice it as separate notes. It becomes a soft, resonant background, with the bass notes giving a quiet foundation.

This is the most common difficulty for adults. The left hand has many more notes than the right, and it is easy to let it become loud and busy. Listen to great recordings and you will hear how quiet the accompaniment is, and how the melody floats above it.

Rubato: freedom with a frame

Rubato means taking flexibility with time: lingering on an important note, moving forward through a phrase, then settling back. Chopin’s music depends on it. But rubato is not the same as being free with everything. Accounts from Chopin’s pupils describe an accompaniment that keeps a steady flow while the melody takes small liberties above it.

For a learner, this is a useful guide. Keep your left hand fundamentally steady. Let the right hand breathe within that frame. Too much rubato sounds sentimental and loses the shape; too little sounds mechanical.

Which nocturnes to start with

Nocturnes vary greatly in difficulty. Some contain passages for advanced pianists. Here are a few that are more approachable, roughly in order:

  • Nocturne in C minor, published posthumously. Short and simple in texture, a good first study in singing tone.
  • Nocturne in E minor, Op. 72 No. 1. An early work with a steady triplet accompaniment and a sad, lyrical melody. A classic starting point.
  • Nocturne in C-sharp minor, published posthumously (Lento con gran espressione). Deeply expressive and widely loved. It contains a few rapid passages that need patient work.
  • Nocturne in E-flat major, Op. 9 No. 2. The most famous of all. The left hand moves widely and the ornaments are delicate, so it is better approached once you have learned one or two of the above.

How to practice a nocturne alone

  • Learn the left hand alone until it is quiet, even, and easy. Practice the jumps slowly, feeling the distance.
  • Sing the right hand before playing it. Decide where each phrase peaks.
  • Practice ornaments slowly and lightly, in the rhythm where they fit, before speeding them up.
  • Listen carefully to the pedal. Nocturnes need pedal, but it must change with the harmony, or the sound becomes muddy.
  • Record and listen for balance. Is the melody always clearly above the accompaniment?

This week, choose one of the first two nocturnes above. Listen to it with the score once, then begin with the left hand alone, very softly. Everything beautiful in a nocturne rests on that quiet foundation.
