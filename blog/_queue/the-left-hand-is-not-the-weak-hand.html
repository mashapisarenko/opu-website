The Left Hand Is Not the Weak Hand

You have spent forty years doing fine work with one hand and carrying things with the other. That is training history, not anatomy, and it can be corrected on purpose.
By Dr. Maria Pisarenko, DMA  ·  November 18, 2026

The left hand gets its own position and its own work from Lesson 5, free: Open the free course →

Ask an adult beginner what is holding them back and a large share will say the same thing: my left hand is weak. They mean it literally — that the hand lacks strength, that it is less capable, that this is a fact about their body they will have to work around.

It is worth taking that belief apart, because it is wrong in a specific and useful way.

What is actually different about your left hand

Your hands have not had the same life. One of them has spent decades writing, using tools, handling small objects with precision. The other has held the paper. The difference you feel at the keyboard is a difference in fine motor training, not in strength — and strength is not what piano playing asks for anyway. It asks for control of a falling arm and independence between fingers, both of which are learned.

This matters because the two diagnoses lead to opposite actions. "My hand is weak" suggests strengthening exercises and grim effort. "My hand is untrained" suggests giving it the practice the other one got, which is what actually works.

There is one real anatomical fact worth knowing: the fourth finger of either hand shares tendons with its neighbors and does not lift independently as well as the others. That is true of both hands and of every pianist who has ever lived. It is managed, not fixed, and it is not a left-hand problem.

The practice habit that keeps it untrained

Here is the trap, and nearly everybody is in it.

In most beginner music, the right hand has the tune and the left hand has something simpler underneath. So when you practice hands separately, the right hand gets the interesting work and the left hand gets a few repetitions of something dull, which you cut short because it is dull. Then you put the hands together and the left hand is the one that falls apart.

You then conclude that the left hand is weak, and the conclusion produces more of the same practice.

The correction is uncomfortable and simple: the left hand needs more time than the right, not less. Twice as many repetitions at least. It is behind because it has been given less, and the way to change that is to give it more.

Five things to do about it

Practice the left hand alone until it is automatic. Not until you can get through it — until you can play it while looking away from the keyboard and counting out loud. If it still needs your attention, it will collapse the moment the right hand needs some.

Give the left hand the melody sometimes. Take a simple tune you already know in the right hand and learn it in the left, an octave or two lower. Nothing exposes how much less your left hand knows, and nothing improves it faster. It is also more interesting than another twenty repetitions of an accompaniment.

Play the accompaniment as if it mattered. Beginners tend to play the left hand carelessly because it is "just" the accompaniment. Evenness in that hand is what makes a piece sound settled. Listen to the left hand alone as a piece of music — is it even, is the pulse steady, do the notes sound at the volume you intended?

Watch the wrist, not the fingers. A left hand that is working too hard usually shows it in a stiff, low wrist and a thumb that grips. The sound goes hard and the hand tires within a minute. The arm should stay heavy and the wrist free — the same rule as the right hand, but nobody ever watches the left one to check.

Do not let it play quietly by accident. Once your left hand is solid, it will often need to be quieter than the right — but that must be a decision, not the residue of weakness. There is a real difference between an accompaniment that is deliberately underneath and one that is merely timid, and it is audible.

What changes when it stops being the weak hand

Two things, and adults are usually surprised by both.

Pieces get easier faster, because the left hand stops being the part that needs three times as many attempts. And the whole sound settles: a confident, even left hand is most of what makes playing sound grounded rather than tentative, and listeners hear that long before they hear anything about the melody.

The part you cannot see

You cannot watch your own left wrist while you are reading music with your eyes and following the right hand with your attention. Stiffness there is nearly always invisible to the person it belongs to, and it is completely obvious from the side.

So put the phone at the side of the keyboard, at about the height of the keys, play a passage where the left hand accompanies, and send it. The written feedback is free, and this is one of the faults it identifies most reliably.
