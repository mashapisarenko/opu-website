Haydn: The Composer Adult Pianists Overlook

Haydn's keyboard sonatas are clear, witty and full of surprises, and this article explains why they suit adults returning to the piano and how to begin them.
By Dr. Maria Pisarenko, DMA  ·  January 2, 2027

Taught by Dr. Maria Pisarenko, DMA — the complete beginner course is free: Start at Lesson 1 →

Ask an adult which classical composers they would like to play, and the answers are usually the same: Bach, Mozart, Beethoven, Chopin. Haydn is almost never on the list. That is a pity, because for an adult returning to the piano, Haydn may be the most rewarding composer of all.

Who Haydn was

Joseph Haydn worked for many years as a court musician for the Esterházy family, writing music on demand for their household. He was an older friend of Mozart and, for a time, a teacher of the young Beethoven. He wrote more than a hundred symphonies, a large body of string quartets, and more than fifty sonatas for keyboard. Many of the forms that Mozart and Beethoven used, Haydn helped to shape.

He was also famous for his humor. His “Surprise” Symphony owes its nickname to a sudden loud chord in the middle of a quiet slow movement. The same playfulness runs through his piano music.

Why Haydn suits returning adults

The texture is clear. Much of Haydn’s piano writing has a melody in the right hand and a light accompaniment in the left. There are few thick chords and few long stretches. You can hear everything you play, which means you can judge it yourself without a teacher beside you.

The movements are short. Many Haydn sonatas have three movements, and some of those movements last only two or three minutes. You can learn one movement as a complete piece, finish it, and feel the satisfaction of having played real concert music from beginning to end.

The forms are easy to follow. Haydn states an idea, develops it, and brings it back. Once you see the plan on the page, memory becomes easier. You know where you are and where you are going.

The wit teaches timing. Haydn’s jokes are made of rests, sudden pauses, unexpected loud chords, and phrases that end a beat early. To make them work you have to count precisely and hold the silences. There is no better training for rhythm.

The writing teaches articulation. Haydn marks slurs, staccatos and accents carefully. Following them exactly develops a precision in the fingers that serves you in every other style.

Where to begin

Haydn’s earlier sonatas, sometimes published under the title of divertimento or partita, are the most approachable. They are short, with minuets and simple fast movements, and many are within reach of an adult after a year or two of steady work. Look in a good collection of his easier sonatas and play through the first pages of several before choosing.

From there, the well-known Sonata in C major, Hob. XVI:35, and the Sonata in D major, Hob. XVI:37, are frequent goals for intermediate players. The “Hob.” refers to the Hoboken catalog, which numbers Haydn’s works. Use those numbers when you search for scores and recordings, since the order of sonatas differs between editions.

Minuets are a particularly good starting point. Haydn wrote many of them inside his sonatas, and each is a complete little piece in three: a minuet, a contrasting trio, and the minuet again.

How to practice Haydn on your own

  • Observe every marking. Before you play, go through the first line with a pencil and note each slur, staccato and dynamic. Then play only what is written.
  • Keep the sound light. Haydn wrote for instruments much lighter than a modern piano. Play with less weight than you would use for Chopin or Brahms, especially in the left hand.
  • Count the rests aloud. Haydn’s silences are part of the joke. If you cut them short, the surprise disappears.
  • Practice the fast passages slowly and evenly. Scales and broken chords are everywhere. Play them at half tempo with a metronome until each note is the same size, then raise the speed in small steps.
  • Listen for the surprise. When you find a sudden loud chord, a pause, or a wrong-key turn, ask yourself what Haydn expected the listener to feel. Then play it so that someone would notice.

The reward

Haydn gives an adult what is hardest to find in piano music: real works by a great composer, short enough to finish, clear enough to hear, and alive with personality. Played well, they sound fresh and a little mischievous, never like exercises. Many students come to Haydn late and wish they had found him sooner.
