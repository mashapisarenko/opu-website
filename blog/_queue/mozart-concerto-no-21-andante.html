Mozart Concerto No. 21 Andante: Why Simple Is Hard

The Andante from Mozart's Piano Concerto No. 21 is one of the best-known melodies in music. Here is why it is famous, and why simplicity is the hardest thing to play.
By Dr. Maria Pisarenko, DMA  ·  December 3, 2026

Taught by Dr. Maria Pisarenko, DMA — the complete beginner course is free: Start at Lesson 1 →

Many people who have never been to a concert know the slow movement of Mozart’s Piano Concerto No. 21 in C major, K. 467. It has been used in films, advertisements and countless recordings. Its popularity owes a great deal to its use in the 1967 Swedish film Elvira Madigan, which is why the concerto is sometimes called by that name. But the music would be famous without the film. I have performed this concerto, and the Andante is one of the most beautiful and most difficult things in it.

What you hear

The movement begins with the orchestra alone. The strings are muted, which gives them a veiled, soft sound. Under the melody, the strings play a gentle, continuous accompaniment in triplets, while the lower strings play plucked notes. The effect is of something floating, suspended, almost weightless.

Then the piano enters with the melody. It is long and arching, with wide leaps and moments where the line rises unexpectedly high. There are dissonant notes, gently clashing with the harmony, that give the melody a touch of longing. It feels simple, but it is never plain.

Why a simple melody is hard

Pianists often say that slow Mozart is harder than fast Mozart, and there is truth in that. In a fast passage, small imperfections pass quickly. In a slow melody, every note is exposed. The audience hears exactly how each note begins, how long it lasts and how it connects to the next one.

Here are the main challenges.

  • The piano cannot truly sustain. Once a key is struck, the sound begins to fade. A singer or violinist can hold a note at the same strength or even grow it. A pianist cannot. To make a melody sound connected, the pianist must listen to how much the previous note has faded and play the next note at a level that continues the line instead of jumping out.
  • Wide leaps. When the melody leaps, the top note can easily sound too loud or too sudden. It must arrive like a singer reaching a high note gracefully, not like a hammer.
  • Balance with the orchestra. The orchestra here is soft, so the piano must be soft as well, yet it must still be the voice the listener follows.
  • Calm timing. The pulse must stay steady and calm. Rushing destroys the sense of floating; dragging makes it heavy.

Singing on the piano

To play this melody well, a pianist has to imagine a voice. Where would a singer breathe? Which note would they lean into, and which would they let fall away? Mozart wrote opera his whole life, and this melody behaves like an operatic aria. Thinking like a singer helps the hands find the right shape.

Touch matters too. The fingers stay close to the keys. The arm is relaxed and supports the hand. The tone should be warm, without a hard edge at the start of each note. Much of this comes not from the fingers but from listening carefully to each sound.

Why this movement matters for you

For an adult learning alone, there is a lesson here that applies from the very first piece. Playing slowly and simply does not mean playing without thought. A five-note melody can be played mechanically or beautifully, and the difference is the same as in Mozart: listening to the end of each note, shaping the line and keeping the accompaniment quieter than the melody.

What you can try this week

  • Listen to the opening of the Andante and notice the muted strings and plucked bass before the piano enters.
  • When the piano enters, sing along quietly with the melody. Notice where you naturally want to breathe.
  • At your piano, play a slow, simple melody you know. On each long note, listen to the sound fading. Play the next note so that it continues the fading sound rather than jumping out above it.
  • Find one leap in your melody and practice arriving on the top note softly and gracefully.
  • Record yourself and listen back. Does it sound like singing, or like separate notes?

This Andante is famous because it seems effortless. That effortlessness is the result of careful listening, and it is something every pianist can work toward.
