"Play It Musically" — What That Actually Means, in Practical Terms

Your ear already knows what beautiful sounds like. The gap is not taste — it is that nobody has told you which physical movements produce it.
By Dr. Maria Pisarenko, DMA  ·  November 16, 2026

Every lesson works on the sound, not only the notes, and the course is free: Open the free course →

Adults who are learning alone tell me a version of this constantly: I can play the notes, but it does not sound like music. They are almost always right about the diagnosis, and almost always wrong about the cause. They think they lack something — feeling, talent, musicality. What they lack is a list of physical actions, because nobody has ever given them one.

"Play it musically" describes a result. It is like telling somebody to be charming. The useful version is a set of things your arm, wrist and fingers actually do, and there are not many of them.

First: your ear is already ahead, and that is the problem

You have listened to music your whole life. You know exactly what a phrase should sound like — which is why your own playing disappoints you so precisely. The ear is not the weak link. The weak link is that the hand has not been taught to produce what the ear already demands, and the distance between the two is the most uncomfortable part of learning as an adult.

It is also the reason this is fixable. You do not need to develop taste. You need technique to catch up with taste you already have.

The four things musical playing is made of

1. Loud comes from a faster arm, not a harder finger. This is the one that changes people's sound in a single session. A loud note is a key that goes down quickly; a soft note is a key that goes down slowly. The speed comes from the weight of the arm arriving, not from squeezing with the finger. Press harder and you get a hard, brittle sound and a tired hand. Let the arm fall and you get volume with warmth. Try one note both ways and listen — the difference is not subtle.

2. One voice is the melody and everything else is quieter. Almost every adult plays both hands at the same volume, because both hands are trying equally hard. A listener then hears a wall. The rule is simple and it must be deliberate: the melody — normally the top notes of the right hand — is clearly louder, and the accompaniment sits underneath. Not slightly. Obviously. Play a passage with the left hand at half its usual volume and it will sound instantly more like a recording than a lesson.

3. A phrase has a shape: it goes somewhere and comes back. Music is not a flat line of equal notes. A phrase rises to a point and relaxes away from it, and the point is usually the highest or longest note. Decide where it is, arrive there a little stronger, and let the notes after it ease off. If you do nothing else from this article, do this: sing the line, notice where your voice naturally leans, and make your hand do what your voice did.

4. The end of a phrase is released, not stopped. Lift the hand at the end of a line, rather than letting the last note simply cease when the next finger arrives. It sounds like a breath, and the absence of those breaths is why continuous playing sounds mechanical even when everything is correct.

What to do at the instrument this week

Take eight bars you can already play without thinking. Not something new — the point is that your hands are free.

Play it four times, changing one thing each time. First, melody clearly louder than the other hand. Second, find the peak of the phrase and lean into it. Third, release the last note of each line as if taking a breath. Fourth, all three together.

This takes five minutes, and it is the single most efficient practice most self-teaching adults never do, because it works on eight bars they can already play instead of on the hard piece.

The thing to stop doing

Do not add feeling by slowing down at random. Expressive playing is not playing with wobbly time — that is the most common overcorrection, and it makes playing sound uncertain rather than expressive. Keep the pulse; shape the sound. Time stays; volume moves.

Why nobody told you this

In a lesson, sound is corrected constantly, in two-second interventions that never make it into a book: lighter left hand — lean on that one — let go at the end of the line. A method book teaches notes because notes can be printed. The sound is taught by ear, by a person listening to you, and the absence of that person is exactly what makes the self-teaching adult's playing sound the way it does.

That absence is fixable at a distance, though — because unlike almost everything else in learning, sound survives a recording.

The part you cannot judge alone

Balance between the hands is the hardest thing to hear from inside your own playing. Nearly every adult who records themselves discovers that the accompaniment is much louder than they believed, and it is the fastest single change available to how they sound.

So record two minutes of something you can already play, with the phone far enough away to hear the room rather than the strings, and send it. The written feedback is free, and balance, phrase shape and release are exactly what it is for.
