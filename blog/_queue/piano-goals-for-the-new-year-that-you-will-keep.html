Piano Goals for the New Year That You Will Actually Keep

"Practice every day" and "learn Clair de Lune" are the two commonest resolutions, and they fail in opposite ways.
By Dr. Maria Pisarenko, DMA  ·  December 28, 2026

A year of lessons, in order, free, is a goal you can actually see the shape of: Start the free course →

Every January a great many adults decide that this is the year they learn the piano. By March most of them have stopped, and it is almost never for lack of wanting it. It is the shape of the goal.

The two commonest resolutions are practice every day and learn a particular famous piece, and they fail in opposite ways.

Why "practice every day" breaks

Daily practice is the right instinct. As a resolution it has a flaw: it can only be broken, never achieved. The first day you miss — and you will, in week two, because of a flight or a cold or a bad day — the record is gone, and a surprising number of people quietly stop altogether rather than restart at one.

Replace it with something that survives a missed day. Five days a week, ten minutes minimum. Two days off are built in, so a missed day is inside the plan instead of a failure of it. Ten minutes is deliberately small: the aim is that on a bad day you sit down anyway, because ten minutes is never unreasonable. Most of the time you will play longer once you have started, and that is the whole trick.

Why "learn Clair de Lune" breaks

Here the flaw is distance. A first-year adult beginner attempting a late-intermediate piece will spend months on something that never sounds like the recording in their head, with no visible progress along the way, and the conclusion they draw is about themselves rather than about the choice of piece.

Keep the piece — it is why you are here, and wanting to play a particular thing is the strongest motivation there is. But put it at the end of a staircase instead of at the top of a wall. A year of steadily harder pieces that you can actually finish will get you there faster than a year of the same three difficult bars, and you will still be playing at the end of it.

Set goals you can check on a Sunday

A usable goal is one where you can tell, without arguing with yourself, whether it happened. Some that work for a first or second year:

Finish a specific number of lessons. "Through Lesson 20 by the end of March." Countable, and it keeps you in order instead of skipping ahead.

Have three pieces you can play from memory at any time. Not new pieces — a small standing repertoire you keep in shape. This is the goal that makes you feel like a person who plays the piano, and almost nobody sets it.

Play for another person four times this year. Once a quarter. It is the most uncomfortable item on the list and the one that changes the most.

Record yourself once a month. Ten monthly recordings is a goal you can verify at a glance, and it is the only way you will ever see your own progress.

Decide now what a bad week looks like

This is the part that is always left out. Write down, in advance, what you do in a week when everything goes wrong: ten minutes, whatever it is, even if it is only scales; no play-throughs; no guilt.

Deciding it in advance matters because in the middle of a bad week you will not be making good decisions about the piano, and without a plan the default is to skip it entirely and then feel bad enough that you skip the next one too. The people who are still playing in December are not the ones who never had a bad week. They are the ones who had already decided what a bad week would look like.

One goal, not six

Finally: choose one. A list of six intentions is a list nobody keeps. Pick the single thing that would make this year feel worthwhile — usually it is being able to sit down and play something, all the way through, for someone you like — and let the practice be whatever serves that.

Write it on a card and put it on the music desk where you will see it in February.
