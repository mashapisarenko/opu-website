Looking Back on a Year of Piano Practice

A practical method for adults learning piano alone to review a year of practice with evidence instead of memory, and to plan the next year from what they find.
By Dr. Maria Pisarenko, DMA  ·  December 27, 2026

Taught by Dr. Maria Pisarenko, DMA — the complete beginner course is free: Start at Lesson 1 →

At the end of a year, most adult learners judge themselves by memory. Memory is a poor witness. It remembers the evening the left hand would not cooperate and forgets the slow weeks when something quietly became easy. Before you plan the next year, I would like you to look at this one with evidence.

Gather what you have

Collect everything that records the year: recordings on your phone, a practice notebook if you kept one, the list of pieces you started, the books you worked through, even the pages you marked in pencil. If you kept nothing, that is your first finding, and it tells you what to change.

The most valuable item is a recording from early in the year. Listen to it, then record the same piece or exercise today. Do not judge the new recording yet. Simply play the two one after the other and listen.

Four honest measures

I ask adults who learn on their own to look at four things, because each can be measured and each matters.

  • Reading. How long does it take you now to work out one new line of music, compared with a year ago? Time it. Faster reading is the quiet engine of everything else.
  • Steadiness. Can you keep a pulse through a whole piece, including the hard bar, without stopping or slowing down? Play with a metronome once and notice where you drift.
  • Sound. In the two recordings, which one is more even? Are your soft notes really soft? Does the melody sound above the accompaniment, or does everything arrive at the same volume?
  • Finishing. How many pieces did you take from the first reading to a version you could play for another person? Starting is easy. Finishing is where real progress lives.

Write down a short answer for each. Numbers where you can, plain sentences where you cannot. “I can read a line of a beginner piece in about two minutes” is useful. “I am bad at reading” is not.

Look at how you practiced, not only what

Progress depends less on talent than on habits, so review the habits. How many days a week did you actually sit down? How long were the sessions? Did you warm up, or start with the hardest passage cold? Did you practice slowly, or play through pieces at full speed and hope?

Be fair to yourself here. Adults practice around jobs, families and tiredness. A year with three short sessions a week, kept steadily, is a good year. A year with bursts of enthusiasm and long silences is the most common pattern I see, and the easiest to fix.

Name what did not work

Every year has a piece that stalled or a method that wasted time. Name it plainly. Perhaps you chose a piece far beyond your reading level and spent months on eight bars. Perhaps you avoided the left hand. Perhaps you only played what you already knew. None of this is failure. It is information, and it is the most valuable part of the review.

Plan the next year from the evidence

Now turn what you found into a plan. Keep it short enough to fit on one page.

  • One skill to strengthen. Choose the weakest of the four measures. If reading was slow, the year’s first goal is reading every day, even for five minutes.
  • Two or three pieces to finish. Not ten. Choose pieces slightly above your current level and decide to bring them to a playable state.
  • A practice rhythm you can keep. Choose days and a length you can sustain in your worst month, not your best. You can always do more.
  • Four checkpoints. Once a season, record yourself and repeat the four measures. This turns a vague hope into a series of small, visible results.

Keep the record going

The single most useful habit you can begin now is recording. A one-minute recording once a week, labeled with the date, will give you next December the evidence you were missing this year. You will hear the improvement your memory refuses to notice, and you will practice with more confidence because of it.

Before you close the year, play one thing you could not play twelve months ago. Play it slowly, with your best sound, and listen to it to the end. That is the honest measure, and it is usually better than you expected.
