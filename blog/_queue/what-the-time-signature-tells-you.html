What the Time Signature Is Actually Telling You

It is not a fraction and it is not decoration. It tells you how to count, and counting is what holds playing together.
By Dr. Maria Pisarenko, DMA  ·  November 23, 2026

The time signature arrives in Lesson 6, after the rhythm it names, and the course is free: Open the free course →

At the very start of a piece, just after the clef, there are two numbers stacked on top of each other. Most adults teaching themselves learn to ignore them within a week, because nothing obviously breaks if you do. The notes are still the notes. The piece still comes out roughly right.

What those two numbers are doing, though, is telling you the one thing that makes playing sound organized instead of approximate — and once you know what they say, a good deal of other music theory stops being mysterious.

The two numbers, in plain words

The top number says how many beats are in each measure. Four means you count to four and start again. Three means you count to three.

The bottom number says which kind of note gets one beat. A 4 there means the quarter note is the beat. That is by far the most common case, and for your first year it is nearly the only one you need.

So four-four means: four beats to a measure, and a quarter note is one beat. Three-four means: three beats to a measure, and a quarter note is still one beat.

It is not a fraction. Nothing is being divided. It is two separate pieces of information stacked for convenience, and reading it as a fraction is why it confuses people for longer than it should.

What actually changes in the sound

Here is the part that matters more than the arithmetic: the first beat of every measure is the strong one. Not louder in a way anybody would describe as loud — leaned on, arrived at, the place the music settles before setting off again.

That is why three-four sounds like a waltz and four-four sounds like walking. Same notes, same speed, different grouping, completely different character. The bar line is not a piece of bookkeeping; it is the reason the music has a shape you can feel.

Which means that when you count out loud — and I hope you are — one is not merely the next number. It is the arrival.

Why it is printed only once

The time signature appears at the beginning and then not again, unless the piece changes it. This surprises beginners, who expect to be reminded. It is not repeated because it is not a marking about a note; it is a statement about how the whole piece is organized, and once said, it stands.

Practical consequence: if you have lost the pulse in the middle of a page, go back to the top and look at it again. A surprising number of "this rhythm makes no sense" problems are an adult counting four in a piece that is in three.

The one that trips everybody: the pickup

Plenty of pieces do not begin on beat one. The first measure is short — a single note, or two — and the music starts on the last beat of an imaginary bar before it. This is a pickup, and it is why counting yourself in matters.

Count a full measure out loud before you play, and come in where the notes say. Start the piece on beat one when it was written to start on beat four and everything after it sits in the wrong place, which the ear registers as the whole performance being slightly off even though every note is correct.

How to feel it rather than calculate it

Count out loud from the first day of a piece, not from the day it gets difficult.

Tap the strong beat with your foot and count the rest. One foot tap per measure, on beat one. If the tapping and the music stop agreeing, you have found where the time goes wrong, which is usually a held note that got cut short.

Listen for the grouping in music you already know. Put on something familiar and find the one. Most popular music is in four; most waltzes, and a great deal of older music, are in three. Once you can hear the grouping in recordings, reading it on the page becomes obvious rather than technical.

Do not change the speed at the bar line. A common fault is a small hesitation before every one, as if gathering for the next measure. It makes the pulse limp. The bar line is not a pause; it is a marker.

What comes later

Other time signatures exist and they are not urgent. Two-four is four-four's smaller sibling and behaves the same way. Six-eight groups differently and feels like a swing rather than a march, and it is worth meeting after the first two are solid rather than alongside them.

There is no advantage in learning all of them early. There is a real disadvantage in learning them before you can hold a steady four.

The part you cannot judge alone

Whether your strong beats are actually where you think they are is very hard to hear while playing, because you already know where they are supposed to be and your ear obligingly puts them there.

So record two minutes, counting out loud, with the phone at the side of the keyboard, and send it. The written feedback is free, and misplaced emphasis is one of the faults it names most clearly — it is also one of the quickest to fix once somebody points at it.
