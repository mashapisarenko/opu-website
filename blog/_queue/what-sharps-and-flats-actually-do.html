What Sharps and Flats Actually Do (and Why One Key Has Two Names)

The rule takes ten seconds to learn. What nobody tells adult beginners is how long a sharp stays in force, and why F sharp and G flat are the same key with two names.
By Dr. Maria Pisarenko, DMA  ·  December 7, 2026

Sharps arrive in Lesson 17 of the free course, in order, after the notes they modify: Start the free course →

There are two halves to the sharp sign. Almost everyone who teaches themselves learns the first half in about ten seconds and never hears the second, and the second is the one that produces wrong notes for months.

Here is the first half. A sharp (♯) in front of a note means: play the very next key to the right. A flat (♭) means: play the very next key to the left. That is the entire rule.

The next key, not the next black key

Read that again, because one word in it does most of the work. The next key. Not the next black key — the next key, whichever color it happens to be.

Most of the time the next key up does happen to be black, which is why the black keys end up carrying the sharp names: C sharp, D sharp, F sharp, G sharp, A sharp. But look at your keyboard between E and F, and between B and C. There is nothing in between. So E sharp is F, and B sharp is C, and neither of those is a trick question — it is simply what the rule produces when there is no black key to land on.

Adults tie themselves in knots here because they try to do it in their heads: counting half steps, picturing the keyboard, converting. Do not. Put your finger on the note and move it to the key immediately beside it. The answer is sitting under your hand.

Why one key has two names

The black key between F and G can be reached from either side. Come up from F and it is F sharp. Come down from G and it is G flat. Same key, same sound, two names — and which name the music uses is not arbitrary, it tells you where the note is going.

You do not need the theory behind that yet. What you need is to stop being startled by it. When you meet G flat in a piece six months from now, your reaction should be that is the black key below G, not this is a new note I have not learned.

The half that causes wrong notes

Here is the part that gets left out. When a sharp appears in front of a note, it stays in force for that note for the rest of the measure. Not just the note you are looking at. Every time that note comes round again inside the same bar, it is still sharp, even though the sign is printed only once.

This is why a passage can sound right the first time and wrong two seconds later. The first F sharp you play correctly because you can see the sign. The second F in the bar has no sign in front of it, so you play F natural, and the bar sours. You then check the notes, find nothing wrong, and conclude that your ear is unreliable. Your ear was right.

The bar line cancels it. New measure, clean slate — unless the key signature says otherwise, which is a subject for later.

How to practice this so it becomes automatic

Do not try to memorize the rule harder. Do this instead, on any piece that has accidentals in it:

Before you play, circle them. Take a pencil and circle every sharp and every flat on the page. Then circle every later occurrence of that same note inside the same measure — the ones with no sign in front of them that are sharp anyway. Those second circles are the notes you would otherwise get wrong.

It takes two minutes and it does something better than memorization: it makes the rule visible in the one place it matters, which is the page in front of you. After a few pieces you will stop needing the pencil, because you will have started seeing the bar rather than the note.

Say the name out loud

One more habit, and it is the one I give every adult student. When you play an accidental, say it: F sharp. Out loud, at the speed you are playing, as you play it.

Saying it out loud is not a memory trick. It is a test. You find out instantly whether you actually read the sign or whether your finger went where it went last time. Silent practice hides that from you; speaking does not let you hide.

Sharps and flats feel like the moment piano notation turns complicated. They are not. They are one rule about the key next door, plus one rule about how long it lasts, and the second one is the only one worth practicing.
