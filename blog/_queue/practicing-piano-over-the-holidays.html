How to Keep Playing Through a Week When Nothing Is Normal

The adults who lose months are almost never the ones who missed four days. They are the ones who decided that missing four days meant they had stopped.
By Dr. Maria Pisarenko, DMA  ·  November 25, 2026

When you come back, go back a few lessons — they are free and they are in order: Open the free course →

A holiday week does something specific to adult beginners, and it is worth naming before it happens rather than afterward.

The instrument is not the problem. Four missed days do very little to your hands. What does damage is the story that gets attached to the four days: I have fallen off, I was not serious, this is what always happens. That story is what turns a short break into a three-month absence, and it is entirely optional.

So here is how to get through a disrupted week without paying for it in January.

Decide in advance what counts as a day of practice

The single most useful thing you can do before a busy week is lower the bar on purpose, in advance, while you are still calm.

Five minutes counts. Not as a consolation — as the real target for this week. Five minutes keeps the habit's thread intact, and the thread is the thing that is hard to rebuild. The hands can lose a week; the routine cannot easily lose a month.

Write it down if that helps: this week, five minutes is a full session. Decided beforehand, it feels like a plan. Decided afterward, the same five minutes feels like failure, which is the same five minutes with worse consequences.

What to play in five minutes

Not the hard passage. Difficult work needs attention you will not have, and a frustrating five minutes is worse than none.

Play something you already know, slowly, listening to the sound. One piece, or eight bars of one. Arm heavy, hand quiet, one thing to listen for — evenness, or the melody sitting above the other hand. You are keeping the physical memory warm and reminding yourself why you like doing this. That is the whole job this week.

If you have ten minutes, add two minutes of reading something far too easy. It is the least demanding useful thing there is.

If there are people in the house

Two practical notes, because this is what actually stops people.

Headphones exist for exactly this week. If you have a digital instrument, the difference between playing and not playing is often nothing more than not wanting to be heard by relatives in the next room.

Early is more reliable than later. Whatever time you protect before the day starts moving is yours. Time you plan for the evening of a holiday week belongs to everybody else.

And if somebody asks you to play

This happens at gatherings, and for an adult who has been learning quietly for a few months it can be the most exposed moment of the year.

You are allowed to say no. You are also allowed to play something well within your reach rather than the difficult piece you have been working on — the short, easy thing you can play while slightly nervous is the right choice, and nobody in the room is comparing you to a recording. They are pleased that someone is playing at all.

If you do play, play slower than you think you need to. Nerves speed everything up, and the single most common cause of a public stumble is a tempo nobody chose.

Coming back afterward

Expect the first session back to be worse than the last one before the break. This is normal, it is temporary, and it is not evidence of anything. The hands recover within a few days.

Make the first session back deliberately easy. Something you can play. Ten minutes. The aim is to reopen the door, not to catch up — there is nothing to catch up, because this is not a race with a schedule attached.

Go back a few steps rather than forward. Replaying a piece from a month ago, well, is a better return than attacking the one you left half-learned. It also shows you something the day-to-day hides: the old piece is easier than it was.

The thing worth remembering

Adults quit in January far more often than in November, and almost never because of the break itself. They quit because they came back to the hardest thing they had been working on, found it in pieces, and concluded that the break had undone months of work.

It had not. It had undone four days.

If you want a second opinion when you return

Coming back after a gap is one of the best moments to have somebody look at your playing, because the habits you built before the break are still there and you are hearing yourself with slightly fresher ears.

Record two or three minutes with the phone at the side of the keyboard and send it. The written feedback is free, and it is a straightforward account of what your hands are doing — which is more useful after a break than any resolution about practicing more.
