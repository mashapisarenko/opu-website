Rachmaninoff's Preludes: A Listening Guide

Rachmaninoff wrote twenty-four preludes, from the bell-like C-sharp minor to the marching G minor; this guide shows an adult listener what to hear in them.
By Dr. Maria Pisarenko, DMA  ·  December 22, 2026

Taught by Dr. Maria Pisarenko, DMA — the complete beginner course is free: Start at Lesson 1 →

Rachmaninoff was one of the great pianists of his century as well as a composer, and his preludes show both sides of him at once. They are short, often only a few minutes long, yet each one feels like a complete world. For an adult who loves the piano, they are among the most rewarding pieces to get to know as a listener, even long before you could think of playing them.

Twenty-four preludes

Rachmaninoff wrote twenty-four preludes in all, one in every major and minor key, following a tradition that goes back to Bach and Chopin. They did not come as a single set. The first, in C-sharp minor, is part of an early collection, his Op. 3. Ten more followed as Op. 23, and the final thirteen as Op. 32. Together they cover all twenty-four keys.

The famous one: C-sharp minor, Op. 3 No. 2

This prelude made Rachmaninoff famous when he was still young, and it followed him for the rest of his life. Audiences asked for it so often that it became almost his signature.

What to listen for:

  • The three-note opening. Three heavy octaves descend in the bass, like the toll of a great bell. That small motif returns throughout the piece and holds it together.
  • Quiet after the bells. After the opening, the music falls to a hush, with soft chords that seem to echo the bells in the distance.
  • The storm in the middle. A fast, agitated section of rushing notes breaks out, building tension until it collapses back into the opening.
  • The return. The opening material comes back enormously expanded, written on four staves, with huge chords across the keyboard. Then it fades into silence.

Listen for the bell sounds. Russian church bells were part of the sound world Rachmaninoff grew up in, and you hear them in many of his works. In this prelude the piano becomes a bell tower.

The march: G minor, Op. 23 No. 5

This is perhaps the second most famous of the preludes. It opens with a sharp, marching rhythm in chords, full of energy and defiance. Then comes a complete contrast: a long, slow, singing melody over a gently rippling accompaniment, as if the march has paused for a memory. Finally the march returns and ends with a few quiet notes rather than a crash.

Listen for how the pianist handles that contrast. In the march, the rhythm must be precise and springy, never heavy. In the middle section, the melody must sing over the moving left hand without being covered. Hearing both in one piece is a lesson in how much range the piano has.

Two more worth knowing

  • D major, Op. 23 No. 4. Lyrical and calm, with a melody that floats over flowing accompaniment. It shows the tender side of Rachmaninoff.
  • G major, Op. 32 No. 5. Short, serene and transparent, with a melody that seems suspended over a gentle shimmer. Many listeners find it one of his most beautiful pages.

Why they matter to you

Rachmaninoff’s preludes are advanced pieces. Their textures are thick, their stretches wide and their demands on strength and control considerable. They are not repertoire for a beginner’s first years. But they teach a listener three things that matter at every level.

  • Melody comes first. Even in the densest passages, there is a singing line. A good pianist never lets you lose it.
  • Sound has weight. Rachmaninoff’s loudest chords are full and deep, not harsh. That comes from the arm and back, not from hitting the keys.
  • Contrast is meaning. The power of these pieces comes from the change between storm and calm. Your own simple pieces benefit from the same thinking.

This week at the piano

Listen to the C-sharp minor Prelude and the G minor Prelude, each twice. Then go to the piano and play three low octaves, or three low single notes if octaves are too wide, letting each one ring fully, with the weight of your relaxed arm rather than a stiff finger. Listen to how long the sound lasts. That full, unforced tone is the beginning of Rachmaninoff’s sound, and you can start building it now.
