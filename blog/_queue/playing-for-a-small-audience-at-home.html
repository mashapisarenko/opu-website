Playing for a Small Audience at Home

Playing for one or two people at home is the gentlest way to start performing, and with the right piece and a little preparation it becomes something to enjoy.
By Dr. Maria Pisarenko, DMA  ·  January 21, 2027

Taught by Dr. Maria Pisarenko, DMA — the complete beginner course is free: Start at Lesson 1 →

Many adults who learn the piano alone have never played for anyone. They practice in the evening when the house is quiet, and if someone walks into the room, they stop. I understand this completely. Playing for others feels exposing, especially when you are not sure your playing is good enough.

But a piece that has never been played for anyone is not quite finished. Something changes the first time another person listens. You hear your own playing differently, you discover which passages are truly secure, and you begin to understand music as communication rather than private exercise. The gentlest place to start is at home, for one or two people you trust.

Why a tiny audience is the right start

As a concert pianist, I know that nerves do not disappear with experience; you learn to work with them. For an adult beginner, the goal is not to eliminate nerves but to meet them in small, manageable doses. A single friendly listener in your own living room is exactly that dose. The piano is familiar, the room is familiar, and nothing is at stake except the experience itself.

Choosing what to play

This is the most important decision, and the most common mistake is choosing the piece you are proudest of. Choose instead the piece you can play most comfortably.

  • Pick something below your current limit. Nerves take away a little control. A piece that is just barely in your hands at home will fall apart in front of someone. A piece that feels easy will survive.
  • Keep it short. One or two minutes is plenty. A short piece played with care is a better experience for you and the listener than a long one played anxiously.
  • Prefer clear character. A simple piece with a definite mood, gentle, cheerful, or sad, gives the listener something to hold onto and gives you something to think about besides the notes.
  • Avoid the passage that always breaks down. If a piece has a spot you have never solved, choose another piece for now.

Preparing the week before

Preparation for playing in front of others is different from ordinary practice. You need to rehearse performing, not only playing.

  • Play it through without stopping once or twice a day, whatever happens. If you slip, keep going. This is the single most useful habit.
  • Practice starting. Sit down, take a breath, hear the first bar in your head, and only then begin. Do this several times a day.
  • Know a few restart points. Mark two or three places in the score where you could pick up again if you lose your way.
  • Record a run-through on your phone. The recording is a small audience of its own, and it raises the stakes just enough.
  • Play it cold at least once, without warming up, the way you might have to when your listener arrives.

On the day

Keep the occasion simple. You do not need to announce a performance. You can say, “I’ve been learning something, may I play it for you?” Let your listener sit somewhere comfortable, not right beside you staring at your hands.

Before you begin, sit for a moment. Adjust the bench, put your hands in your lap, and breathe. Think of the character of the opening, not of the difficult passage halfway through. Then begin slower than you think you need. Nerves make almost everyone rush.

When something goes wrong

Something will probably go wrong. That is fine. Your listener is not checking notes against a score. They are listening to music and to you. If you slip, keep the pulse and carry on. If you lose your place, go to one of your marked restart points calmly. Do not apologize in the middle. Finish the piece, let the last sound fade, and then lift your hands.

Afterward, resist the urge to list every mistake aloud. Simply say thank you. Later, alone, write down what went well and one thing to work on.

Building from here

Once you have played for one person, play for them again in a few weeks, or for someone new. Each time it gets a little easier. Over months, you build something valuable: the knowledge that you can share music, imperfectly and honestly, and that people are glad to hear it.

Choose your piece today, the easiest one you love, and play it through once without stopping. Then decide who you would like to play it for.
