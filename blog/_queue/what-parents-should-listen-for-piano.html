What a Parent Should Actually Listen For in a Child's First Year of Piano

Parents ask me whether their child is doing well. The honest answer has almost nothing to do with how many pieces they can play, and everything to do with four quieter things.
By Dr. Maria Pisarenko, DMA  ·  October 16, 2026

The free course is written for adults, but the ear work in Lesson 1 is the same at any age: Start at Lesson 1 →

A parent asked me last year whether her daughter was behind. The other child in their class had learned six pieces that term; hers had learned three. I asked her to play me both. The girl who had learned three was the one I would have bet on, and I want to explain why, because the thing parents are given to measure is almost never the thing that matters.

Pieces learned is a countable number, and countable numbers are comforting. But a piece can be learned by understanding it or by memorizing the sequence of finger movements that produce it, and from the outside those two look identical for about a year. Then they stop looking identical, sharply, and the child who memorized movements hits a wall that nobody can explain to them.

Here is what I listen for instead.

1. Can they find a tune by ear?

Ask your child to play Happy Birthday, or anything they know, without music in front of them. Not well — just to find it, slowly, hunting for the next note and correcting themselves when it is wrong.

A child who can do this has connected the sound in their head to the keys under their hand. That connection is the whole instrument. A child who cannot, after a year of lessons, has been taught to convert symbols into finger movements without the sound ever passing through, and that is the most common and most damaging gap in beginner teaching.

It is also completely fixable, at any point, by asking for it. Children find tunes by ear readily when someone tells them it is allowed.

2. Do they play the same phrase twice in a row the same way?

Not the same as the recording. The same as themselves, twice.

Consistency means the child has a mental model of the passage. Inconsistency — a different note wrong each time, a different hesitation — means they are reconstructing it from scratch on every attempt, which is exhausting and does not accumulate. This is the single most useful thing a non-musician parent can notice, because it needs no musical knowledge at all. You are simply listening for whether it is the same twice.

3. What does their hand look like when they are concentrating?

Watch the moment the music gets harder. Do the fingers flatten out? Do the shoulders rise? Does the wrist drop below the level of the keys?

Everyone tenses under difficulty; that is not the problem. The question is whether the tension releases afterwards or whether it has become the default posture. A child who plays with raised shoulders in month two and still does in month ten has learned the tension along with the notes, and it will cost them years later. Say nothing in the moment — corrections during playing rarely land — but mention it to the teacher.

4. Do they ever play when nobody asked them to?

This is the one that predicts whether they continue.

Not practice. Practice is a duty and duties are negotiated. I mean the five minutes of aimless noise on the way past the instrument — picking out a film theme, banging out something they invented, playing the same four bars eleven times because they like how it sounds. That is a child who has understood that the piano is theirs to use rather than a subject they are being tested on.

If it never happens, the instrument has become homework. That is worth addressing directly, and usually the fix is not more discipline but less: a stretch of time where nothing is being assessed and they are allowed to make a mess.

What I would stop measuring

Speed. Fast playing is a by-product of secure playing, never a route to it. A child pushed to play faster learns to push, and pushing is the habit that has to be undone first in every adult I have ever retaught.

Comparisons with the other child in the class. Children arrive with different amounts of music already in their ears, and that gap closes. What does not close on its own is a missing connection between sound and key.

Grade exams, in the first year. They are a useful external deadline later. Early on they tend to produce three very well-drilled pieces and nothing underneath them.

The question to ask the teacher

Not “how is she doing”, which invites a polite answer. Ask: what is the one thing you are working on with her at the moment, and how will I know when it is solved?

A teacher with a plan answers that immediately and specifically. A teacher without one gives you a general impression. You will learn more from thirty seconds of that answer than from a term of reports.

One last thing

Almost every adult who comes to me having “played as a child and given it up” describes the same ending: it stopped being interesting, and nobody noticed in time. Very few of them stopped because it was too hard.

So if you are going to watch one thing, watch the fourth one. A child who plays when nobody asked is a child who is going to keep playing.
