What a Piano Competition Teaches You About Nerves

Competitions taught Dr. Pisarenko that nerves are information, preparation goes deeper than repetition, and listening beats fear. Here is how adults can use those lessons at home.
By Dr. Maria Pisarenko, DMA  ·  October 31, 2026

Taught by Dr. Maria Pisarenko, DMA — the complete beginner course is free: Start at Lesson 1 →

A competition jury is an unusual kind of audience, and the experience teaches things worth passing on. It did not give me fearlessness. It gave me a way of working with fear. Among my competition results are a second prize at the International Piano Competition in Memory of Sviatoslav Richter in Paris, a second prize at the Frederic Chopin International Competition in Rome, and top honors at the First International Tchaikovsky Youth Competition in Moscow, which opened the door to the Central Music School of the Moscow Tchaikovsky Conservatory. I mention them not to impress you but because they taught me three things that matter to every adult who has ever frozen when someone started listening.

Lesson one: nerves are information, not a verdict

Before a competition performance the body does what it always does when it senses an audience: the heart speeds up, the hands go cold, the breath turns shallow. Young competitors learn very quickly that this reaction cannot be argued away. What you can do is give it a job. A shaking hand cannot negotiate with a phrase, but it can follow a clear plan for the first bar.

That is why serious competitors do not walk onstage hoping to feel calm. They walk on with the beginning decided: the tempo, the weight of the first note, the way the arm will travel. The nerves are still there, but they have something to hold on to.

Lesson two: preparation means more than repetition

Many adults prepare a piece by playing it again and again until it “feels safe.” Competition preparation is different, because the performance has to survive conditions you cannot control: an unfamiliar piano, a cold hall, a long wait backstage. So the preparation has to be deeper than the fingers.

  • Know the piece away from the keyboard. Can you sing the melody? Can you say what happens in the harmony at the turn of the phrase? If you can, a slip in the hands will not erase the piece.
  • Practice starting in the middle. Begin at a phrase you choose at random. Real performances rarely go as rehearsed, and the ability to rejoin the music is worth more than perfection from bar one.
  • Play it for someone, early. Do not wait until you feel ready. Playing for a listener while the piece is still imperfect teaches you what pressure does to your tempo and your sound.

Lesson three: the real skill is listening

This is the one people do not expect. When a competition goes badly, it is almost never because the pianist lacked notes. It is because the pianist stopped listening to the sound and started listening to the fear. Attention narrows to “am I going to make a mistake?” and the piano, which is the one thing that can actually tell you what is happening, goes unheard.

The training I received in Russia, and the method I teach from the Alexander Alexandrovich Nikolaev School of Piano Playing, puts the ear first: you hear the sound, then you play it, and you judge the result by what comes out of the instrument, not by watching your hands. That habit is the best protection against panic I know. A mind busy listening has no room left to catastrophize.

Why this matters if you will never enter a competition

You do not need a jury to feel what a jury feels. A sister-in-law walks into the room, a friend asks you to play something, and suddenly the piece you knew yesterday disappears. The mechanism is exactly the same. So the same remedies work.

  • Decide the first four bars completely. Tempo, dynamic, the sound of the very first note. Rehearse only that until it is automatic.
  • Take one slow breath out before you begin. Not a deep breath in, which tends to tighten the shoulders, but a long breath out.
  • Give your attention to the sound. When you feel the fear rise, ask one question: what does the next note sound like? Then answer it with your arm.
  • Expect to make a mistake, and plan to keep going. The audience, whether a jury or your family, remembers whether the music continued, not whether every note was right.
  • Collect small performances. Play for one person, then two, then record yourself. Pressure becomes familiar the way a staircase does: one step at a time.

A kinder way to think about being heard

I have spent my working life teaching adults who were sure it was too late. Almost all of them tell me the same thing: they can play at home, alone, but not when anyone is listening. I do not treat that as a flaw. It is a sign that you care about the music, and caring is exactly what produces nerves. The goal is not to remove the feeling but to build a habit strong enough to play through it.

Competitions gave me that habit early. You can build it later, at your own pace, in your own living room.
