How Loud Is Loud? p, mf and f, Explained Practically

There is no decibel value for forte. What there is, is a relationship — and once you see it, the markings stop being guesswork.
By Dr. Maria Pisarenko, DMA  ·  January 6, 2027

The letters under the staff are Italian abbreviations. p is piano, soft. f is forte, loud. mp and mf are mezzo — moderately — soft and loud. Add letters to go further: pp is very soft, ff very loud.

That much every method book says. What almost none of them say is the part that actually causes the trouble.

They are relative, not absolute

There is no volume that simply is forte. A forte in a quiet, slow piece is not the same sound as a forte in a big loud one. A forte on an upright in a small room is not a forte in a concert hall.

What the marking tells you is where this passage sits compared with the passage next to it. Dynamics are a landscape, not a measurement. The question is never “is this loud enough?” — it is “is this louder than the part before it, by roughly the amount the composer asked for?”

Read them that way and the anxiety goes out of them.

Find your own range first

Do this once, at your own instrument, and you will stop guessing.

Play a single note as softly as you can while still getting a clear sound — not a note that half-speaks, a real one. That is your floor. Now play one as loudly as you can without the tone turning harsh or the instrument banging. That is your ceiling. Listen to the distance between them.

Everything in every piece you play lives between those two sounds. mf is somewhere in the middle. p is near the floor but not on it. Your range is almost certainly wider than you have been using: most adult beginners spend their whole time in a narrow band around mezzo-forte, and the result sounds flat no matter how correct the notes are.

The mistake that makes forte sound bad

Loud is not the same as hard. The most common way an adult plays a forte is by striking from the fingers with a tightened wrist, and the sound comes out harsh and percussive. The piano complains.

Volume comes from arm weight, not finger force. Let more of the arm in behind the key, keep the wrist free, and the sound gets bigger while staying round. A big sound is a relaxed sound. That is counter-intuitive and it is true.

The mistake that makes piano sound bad

The opposite error: playing softly by pressing timidly, so some notes fail to speak at all and the line comes out with holes in it.

Soft still needs the key to go all the way down. Think of soft as controlled rather than weak — the hand stays in contact, the movement is small but definite. A quiet passage in which every note sounds is far more impressive than a loud one.

The twenty-second habit

Before you play a piece, look through it once and find the loudest marking and the softest one. Those two are the ends of your map. Everything else you play between them — and suddenly every marking in the middle has an obvious value.

That is the difference between playing dynamics and playing notes.
