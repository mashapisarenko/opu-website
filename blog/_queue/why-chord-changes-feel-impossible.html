Why Chord Changes Feel Impossible — and the Practice That Fixes Them

The problem is almost never speed. It is that you are moving five fingers when the music only asked you to move one.
By Dr. Maria Pisarenko, DMA  ·  December 9, 2026

Chords arrive in Lesson 11 of the free course, one at a time, with the changes practiced in order: Start the free course →

Everything is going well until the chord changes. The first chord is fine. The second chord, played on its own, is fine. Putting one after the other in time is somehow a different instrument.

This is the most common wall an adult beginner hits, and the reason is almost never what people assume. It is not that your hands are slow, or stiff, or too old. It is that you are moving your whole hand when the music asked you to move one finger.

Find out what actually moves

Take the two chords that are giving you trouble and put them side by side on the keyboard — play the first, then the second, no rhythm, no hurry. Now ask a very specific question: which fingers are on a different key than they were?

Do it note by note. In the left hand, moving from the G chord to the C chord: finger 5 plays G in both chords, so it does not move at all. Finger 2 takes the C. Only finger 1 leaves its place, moving up to E. One finger. That is the entire change, and once you have seen it you cannot unsee it.

Most beginner chord pairs are like this. One or two fingers move and the others are already where they need to be. But if you have never checked, your hand does the safe thing: it lifts off completely, hovers, re-forms the new shape in the air, and lands. That takes four times as long as the music allows, and it is why the change feels impossible.

The common finger is the anchor

A finger that stays put between two chords is not doing nothing. It is the thing your hand navigates by.

Keep it down. Literally — do not lift it. Let the other fingers move around it while that one key stays pressed or lightly touched. The hand now has a fixed point and the change becomes a small adjustment rather than a jump across a gap. This single instruction fixes more chord changes than any amount of repetition.

Practice the change, not the chords

Here is the mistake in how most people practice this. They play the passage, it falls apart at the change, so they go back to the beginning and play the passage again. The chords get better. The change does not, because they have practiced the change once per attempt and the chords twenty times.

Isolate the two chords and nothing else. First chord, second chord, back to the first, again — slowly, for a minute. No rhythm, no counting, no piece around it. Just the crossing. You are not learning the chords. You have those. You are teaching your hand one specific journey.

A minute of that is worth twenty minutes of playing the piece from the top.

Stop watching your hand

Once the change works at all, stop looking at it. Put your eyes on the music and make the change without visual confirmation.

This feels reckless and it is the whole point. A hand that has to be watched cannot play in time, because looking down takes longer than the beat allows, and reading and watching cannot happen at once. The hand learns location far faster than adults expect — but only when it is not being supervised. Give it three days of changing chords with your eyes on the page and you will be startled.

Then, and only then, add the rhythm

When the change happens on its own, put it back into time — slowly. Slowly enough that the change is not rushed, because a change that is rushed is a change that has stopped being learned and started being survived.

The order matters: see what moves, keep the anchor, isolate the crossing, look away, then count. Doing it in that order takes an evening. Doing it in the usual order — playing the piece over and over and hoping — can take months and often ends with somebody deciding they are not musical.

They were musical. They were moving five fingers instead of one.
