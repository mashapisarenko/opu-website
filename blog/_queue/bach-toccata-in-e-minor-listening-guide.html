Bach Toccata in E Minor, BWV 914: A Listening Guide

Bach's Toccata in E minor moves from free improvisation to strict counterpoint and back. Here is how to follow its sections and hear the separate voices.
By Dr. Maria Pisarenko, DMA  ·  November 28, 2026

Taught by Dr. Maria Pisarenko, DMA — the complete beginner course is free: Start at Lesson 1 →

Bach wrote a set of toccatas for keyboard early in his career, and the Toccata in E minor, BWV 914, is one of the most compact and satisfying of them. I have performed it, and I love it because it shows two sides of Bach in a few minutes: the improviser who seems to be inventing music on the spot, and the master of counterpoint who weaves several independent lines together.

What a toccata is

The word toccata comes from the Italian for “to touch.” Originally it described a piece that displayed the player’s hands and the instrument, often with a free, improvised character. Bach’s toccatas mix that free style with strict fugal writing. They are built from contrasting sections, and part of the pleasure is hearing the music change character from one to the next.

This music was written for harpsichord or other keyboard instruments of Bach’s time, not for the modern piano. On the piano, the player has to make choices about sound, touch and pedal that the harpsichordist does not.

The sections

You can hear the toccata as a sequence of four sections.

  • A free opening. The piece begins with a short, improvisatory passage. It sounds like someone trying the instrument, settling into the key, gathering attention.
  • A contrapuntal section. Then the texture becomes organized. Several voices enter and answer one another, each with its own line. The music becomes more measured and serious.
  • An Adagio. Next comes a slow, free section with an expressive, speaking quality, almost like a recitative in an opera, where the singer declaims rather than sings a regular tune. Rich harmonies and pauses give it a sense of grief or searching.
  • The closing fugue. Finally, a quick fugue in three voices. Its subject is a running line of fast notes, full of energy. Each voice enters with that subject in turn, and the music builds momentum to the end.

Listening to counterpoint

For many listeners, counterpoint sounds like a busy blur. The secret is to stop trying to hear everything at once. In a fugue, one melody, the subject, is introduced by one voice alone. Then a second voice enters with the same subject while the first voice continues with something new. Then a third.

When you listen to the closing fugue, try this: follow the subject the first time it appears, on its own. Learn its shape. Then, as the other voices enter, listen for that same shape coming in higher or lower. Every time you recognize it, you are hearing the architecture of the piece.

What it asks of the pianist

On the piano, the main challenge is clarity. Each voice must be heard as its own line, not as part of a mass of notes. That means controlling how loudly each finger plays, keeping the fast notes even, and using the pedal sparingly or not at all in the contrapuntal sections so the lines stay clean.

The Adagio asks for something different: freedom of timing and depth of sound. The pianist must make silence and harmony speak. Moving from the strict fugue to the free Adagio and back again requires a real change of mind, not just a change of speed.

Why this matters for you

Even simple pieces for beginners often have two independent lines, one in each hand. The skill of hearing and shaping more than one line at a time is one of the most important a pianist develops. Bach is the best teacher of that skill, because in his music every line matters.

What you can try this week

  • Listen to the whole toccata once, and try to notice when each new section begins. Write down the four changes you hear.
  • Listen to the closing fugue again and count how many times you recognize the running subject.
  • At the piano, play a simple melody with your right hand while your left hand holds a single note or plays a slow second line. Try to make the right hand clearly louder.
  • Then switch: make the left hand the more important line and the right hand quieter. This is the first step toward playing counterpoint.
  • Practice it slowly, without pedal, listening for whether both lines can be heard.

Bach rewards attention more than any composer I know. The more carefully you listen, the more voices you will hear.
