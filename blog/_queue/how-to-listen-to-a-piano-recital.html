How to Listen to a Piano Recital

A concert pianist explains how to prepare for a piano recital, what to listen for in the hall, the basic etiquette, and how to bring it all back to your practice.
By Dr. Maria Pisarenko, DMA  ·  December 29, 2026

Taught by Dr. Maria Pisarenko, DMA — the complete beginner course is free: Start at Lesson 1 →

If you are learning piano on your own, a live recital is one of the best lessons you can get. For two hours you can watch and hear a trained pianist solve, in real time, the same problems you meet at home: how to make a melody sing, how to keep a pulse, how to shape a phrase. But a recital rewards a prepared listener. Here is how to get the most from one.

Before you go

Look at the program in advance. Most halls publish it. Choose one piece you do not know and listen to a recording of it once or twice. You do not need to study it. You only need enough familiarity that, in the hall, you can listen to how this pianist plays it rather than trying to learn what it is.

If you can, choose a seat where you can see the pianist’s hands, usually on the left side of the hall. Watching the hands is part of the lesson.

The simple etiquette

Recital etiquette exists for one reason: so that everyone can hear. A few points cover almost everything.

  • Arrive early. If you are late, you will usually be asked to wait at the door until a break between pieces.
  • Silence your phone completely. Not on vibrate. A vibrating phone in a quiet hall carries further than you think.
  • Unwrap anything before the music starts. A cough drop opened slowly during a soft passage is heard by everyone.
  • Applaud at the end of the whole work. A sonata or suite has several movements with short pauses between them. Tradition is to hold applause until the last movement ends. If you are unsure, wait until the pianist lowers the hands and turns toward the audience, or until others begin.

No one will scold you for a mistake. But a listening hall feels different from the stage. Silence from the audience is part of the music, and a pianist plays differently when the room is truly quiet.

What to listen for

Do not try to hear everything. Choose one or two things per piece.

The first sound. Notice how the pianist begins. Is there a moment of stillness before the first note? How loud is the first chord, and how does the sound fill the room? The opening tells you a great deal about the performance that follows.

The melody and what is under it. In almost any piece, there is a line that matters most. Listen for how clearly it sounds above the accompaniment. Then listen to the accompaniment itself. In a fine performance it is softer, but never careless.

The shape of phrases. Musical phrases rise and fall like sentences. Listen for where the pianist leans and where the sound relaxes. You will notice that a good phrase rarely stays at one volume.

Contrast. Listen for changes of character: loud and soft, fast and slow, light and heavy. How does the pianist move between them? Abrupt or gradual?

Silence. Notice rests and the pauses between sections. Experienced performers let silence last. It is one of the clearest signs of control.

What to watch

Watch the shoulders and arms, not only the fingers. In loud passages, a good pianist uses the weight of the arm, not tension in the hand. In soft passages, notice how close the fingers stay to the keys. Watch the breathing at the start of phrases. Watch the right foot, and see how often the pedal changes. Many adult learners are surprised by how much the pianist’s body moves, and how little effort it appears to cost.

After the recital

On the way home, write down three things you noticed. Be specific: “The left hand in the waltz was much softer than I play mine” is a note you can use. “It was beautiful” is not.

Bringing it to your practice

The next time you sit at the piano, choose one of those three notes and work on it for a week. If you noticed the stillness before the first chord, begin every piece with two seconds of silence and a breath. If you noticed how soft the accompaniment was, play your left hand alone at half your usual volume, then add the melody on top. If you noticed how the pianist let rests last, count every rest aloud.

Listening in this way trains your ear, and your ear is the teacher you carry with you when you practice alone. Each recital you attend with attention makes that teacher a little sharper.
