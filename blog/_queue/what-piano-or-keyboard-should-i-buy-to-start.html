What Piano or Keyboard Should You Buy to Start? What Actually Matters

One requirement is structural and everything else is preference. Adults put off starting for months over a decision that is simpler than the listings make it look.
By Dr. Maria Pisarenko, DMA  ·  December 2, 2026

The instruments and accessories I actually recommend are on one page: See what I recommend →

A lot of adults put off starting for months over this question, and I understand why. The instrument is the one part of learning the piano that costs real money up front, the advice online contradicts itself, and there is an uncomfortable suspicion that whatever you buy, someone will tell you it was the wrong thing.

So let me separate the part that genuinely affects your learning from the part that is taste, budget, or somebody else's preference.

The one thing that is not negotiable

The keys must be weighted.

This is the whole list. Everything else on this page is a preference; this one is structural.

A weighted key resists your finger and returns. That resistance is what your hand learns against, and almost everything I teach in the first year — arm weight, a firm fingertip, a loose wrist, the difference between a note that sings and a note that is merely correct — depends on the key pushing back. On a keyboard with light springy keys, none of those sensations exist. You can learn where the notes are. You cannot learn to play.

The practical consequence is the one people do not expect: an adult who practices for a year on an unweighted keyboard and then sits at a real piano usually cannot play their own pieces. The notes are there and the hand is not. That is a year of work spent building a technique for a different instrument.

You will see the phrase fully weighted or hammer action in a listing. That is the phrase to look for. Semi-weighted is not the same thing and is not enough.

What matters, in order, after that

Eighty-eight keys. You will not need the top and bottom octaves for a while, and then one day a piece goes there and stops. Sixty-one keys is a real limit you will meet inside the first year. If the choice is between sixty-one weighted keys and eighty-eight unweighted ones, take the sixty-one — weight beats range.

A sustain pedal that is a pedal. The small plastic block that comes in the box with many keyboards works electrically and is unusable musically: it slides on the floor, it has no travel, and it is on or off with nothing in between. A proper piano-style pedal costs very little and it is the single cheapest upgrade available to a beginner.

A bench you can adjust. Not a dining chair. Height is the most common cause of the wrist and forearm trouble adults write to me about, and a fixed chair takes the adjustment out of your hands entirely. An adjustable bench is not a luxury item.

Headphones, if you live near anyone. This is not about the instrument. It is about whether you feel free to make mistakes loudly at ten at night. More adults quit over self-consciousness in a shared apartment than over difficulty.

What does not matter nearly as much as the listings suggest

The number of sounds. Four hundred voices is a marketing number. You will use one.

Recording features, accompaniment styles, built-in lessons. The built-in lesson programs in particular tend to teach note-finding and call it playing. They will not tell you that your wrist is locked, because they cannot see it.

Brand, above a certain floor. Among the established makers at a similar price and specification, the differences are real but they are not what stands between you and playing.

Acoustic or digital

This is a genuine question and it does not have one answer.

An acoustic piano gives a response and a resonance that no digital instrument fully reproduces, and if you have the room, the budget and no neighbors sharing a wall, it is lovely. It also needs tuning, it is a serious object to move, and a cheap or neglected acoustic is worse to learn on than a good digital — an upright with uneven action and notes that do not speak teaches your hand the wrong lesson every day.

A good digital piano with hammer action and a real pedal is an entirely honest instrument to learn on. It never goes out of tune, it has headphones, and it will take you through everything in the first several years. I would rather you started this month on a decent digital than waited two years for the right acoustic.

If you are looking at a secondhand acoustic, take somebody who plays. Play every note. Listen for the ones that do not sound, sound twice, or stay down.

Buy the cheapest instrument that meets the one condition

If money is the constraint — and for most people it is — the decision is simpler than it looks. Find the least expensive instrument with fully weighted hammer-action keys, buy a real pedal and an adjustable bench, and spend nothing else. You will know within a year what you want next, and you will know it from playing rather than from reading.

Then start. And when you want to know what your hands are doing — which is the part no instrument and no app can tell you — record a few minutes with the camera at the side of the keyboard and send it. The written feedback is free, and it is specific.
