How to Practice Slowly Without It Becoming Meaningless

“Practice it slowly” is the most-repeated advice in music and the least explained. There is a right way to do it, and most adults are not doing it.
By Dr. Maria Pisarenko, DMA  ·  January 4, 2027

Every teacher says it. Every book says it. Practice slowly. And then nobody explains what slow practice is for, so adults do the obvious thing — play the same notes at half speed while thinking about something else — and reasonably conclude it does not work.

It does work. But it is not the fast version played slower. It is a different activity.

What slow practice is actually doing

At speed, you cannot observe yourself. The notes arrive faster than attention can follow, so you play on autopilot and hear only the overall result — good or bad — with no information about why.

Slow practice buys you time between notes. In that time you can see where your hand is going next, notice a finger that is tightening, hear whether two notes were actually together. It is not an easier version of the piece. It is the version in which you can still perceive what you are doing.

Which leads straight to the rule that makes or breaks it.

If you are not noticing something, you are not practicing slowly

Slow playing with your attention elsewhere builds nothing. Worse, it builds nothing while feeling virtuous, which is why people do it for weeks and give up on the method.

So give each slow pass one job, out loud if it helps:

This pass I am watching the jump in bar 9.
This pass I am listening for whether the left-hand chord sounds as one sound.
This pass I am checking my shoulders.

One thing per pass. Three slow passes with a job each are worth more than twenty minutes of slow noise.

How slow is slow enough

Here is a test that removes the guesswork. Slow enough that you know the next note before you have to play it.

If you are reading each note at the instant your finger needs it, you are too fast, whatever the metronome says. When you can see ahead, you are at the right speed — and for a hard passage that is often absurdly slow, three or four times slower than the piece. That is fine. It is temporary.

Do not practice slowly with the pedal down

A small thing with a large effect. The sustain pedal covers gaps, so at slow speed it hides exactly the joins you are trying to inspect. Practice slow passages dry, then add the pedal back when the notes are secure.

Coming back up to speed

This is where the work is usually thrown away. People practice slowly for a week and then jump straight to full tempo, the passage falls apart, and they conclude slow practice does not transfer.

Come up in steps, and make each one boringly small — five or six clicks of the metronome at a time. At each new speed, play the passage twice correctly before you move up. If it breaks, go back two steps, not one.

The slowest speed you can play a passage badly is not your speed. The fastest speed at which it is still completely accurate is, and that number moves up on its own over days.

The short version

Slow enough to see ahead. One thing to notice per pass. No pedal. Come up in small steps and only from accuracy. Done that way, a passage that has resisted you for a month usually gives way in a week.
