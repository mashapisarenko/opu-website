Tchaikovsky's Album for the Young, for Adults

Tchaikovsky's Album for the Young, Op. 39, is real concert music in miniature, and this article explains why it suits adult beginners and where to start.
By Dr. Maria Pisarenko, DMA  ·  January 9, 2027

Taught by Dr. Maria Pisarenko, DMA — the complete beginner course is free: Start at Lesson 1 →

Many adults avoid anything with “children” or “young” in the title. They assume it will be simplistic, or written to teach a skill rather than to be music. Tchaikovsky’s Album for the Young, Op. 39, proves the opposite. It is a collection of twenty-four short pieces by one of the great composers, and it holds as much feeling as many longer works. For an adult learning alone, it may be the best first book of real repertoire there is.

What the album is

Tchaikovsky wrote the album in 1878, following the model of Schumann’s Album for the Young, Op. 68. He dedicated it to his young nephew, Vladimir Davydov. The pieces follow a child’s day and imagination: a morning prayer, a winter morning, games with toy soldiers and a hobby horse, a doll that falls ill, a nanny’s tale, the witch Baba Yaga, and a set of songs from Russia, Italy, France, Germany and Naples. The album ends in church.

The titles are childlike. The music is not. “The Sick Doll” is a short study in grief. “Morning Prayer” is a chorale of real depth. “Old French Song” has a melancholy melody that adults often find they cannot forget.

Why these pieces suit adults

They are short. Most pieces are one or two pages. You can learn one in a few weeks, play it from beginning to end, and move on. That rhythm of finishing is what keeps adults practicing.

They are complete music. Each piece has a beginning, a high point and an ending. Nothing is a fragment or an exercise. When you can play one, you can play it for someone and it will sound like music.

They speak to adult emotions. Nostalgia, tenderness, sadness, playfulness, quiet faith: these are feelings an adult brings to the piano and wants to express. Tchaikovsky gives each one a small, perfect form.

Each piece teaches something specific. One trains you to voice chords, another to keep a march rhythm steady, another to shape a long melody over an accompaniment. You learn technique inside music rather than apart from it.

They prepare you for more. The harmony, phrasing and character of these pieces lead directly toward Tchaikovsky’s The Seasons and toward Russian and Romantic repertoire in general.

A note on difficulty

The album is not for your first month. Most pieces are early intermediate. You will want to be comfortable reading in both clefs, playing hands together, and using the pedal in a simple way. If you are still at the very beginning, keep the album in sight as a goal. If you have been playing steadily for a year or more, you can begin now.

The order of the pieces differs between editions, so search by title rather than by number.

Where to start

These pieces are, in my experience as a teacher, good first choices for adults.

  • “Old French Song.” A simple, beautiful melody over a quiet accompaniment. It teaches you to make the right hand sing while the left stays in the background.
  • “Morning Prayer.” Slow chords in several voices, like a small choir. It teaches you to hold notes for their full length and to listen to how chords connect.
  • “The Sick Doll.” Sighing two-note figures and long silences. It teaches you to shape a phrase that falls, and to let rests speak.
  • “Italian Song.” A light, lilting tune in three. It teaches a graceful waltz-like accompaniment.
  • “March of the Wooden Soldiers.” Crisp dotted rhythms and staccato chords. It teaches steady rhythm and precise, light articulation.

Once these feel secure, the “Waltz,” “Mazurka” and “Sweet Dream” are natural next steps.

How to practice them alone

  • Listen first. Find a good recording of the whole album and listen to the piece you are learning several times before you play a note. Know how it should sound.
  • Read the title as an instruction. Ask what the title tells you about tempo, sound and mood. A sick doll is not played loudly. A soldier does not stroll.
  • Hands separately, slowly. Learn each hand alone until it is secure, then put them together at a slow tempo.
  • Find the melody. In almost every piece, one line matters most. Mark it in pencil and practice making it sing above the rest.
  • Finish it. When a piece is playable, record it. Then begin the next one. Over a year or two, you can build a small repertoire of real music that you love and can play for others.

Tchaikovsky wrote these pieces for a child, but he wrote them as a master. Adults who play them often discover that they hear things in this music that no child could.
