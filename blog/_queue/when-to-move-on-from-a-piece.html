When Should You Move On From a Piece?

You do not have to finish a piece to have learned from it. Knowing when to let one go is a skill of its own.
By Dr. Maria Pisarenko, DMA  ·  January 27, 2027

The free course moves in order, so you always know what comes next: Start the free course →

Adults working alone almost always keep a piece too long. There is no teacher to say “good, take this next,” so the piece stays open for months, gradually stops improving, and quietly turns into the reason practice has become dull.

The opposite error exists too — abandoning everything at the halfway point and never finishing anything — but it is much less common in adults, who tend to be conscientious to their own cost.

The honest test

Ask one question: has this piece improved in the last two weeks?

Not “is it perfect.” Not “could it be better” — everything could be better. Has it actually moved in fourteen days of practice?

If it has not, the piece has taught you what it has to teach. Keeping it open past that point is repetition, not learning.

Four signs it is time

You can play it through at tempo with the right notes, and the mistakes that remain are different every time. Random mistakes are a concentration matter, not a learning one, and they do not respond to more repetition.

You are bored of it. This is a legitimate reason and adults dismiss it as laziness. It is not: a bored player stops listening, and practice without listening does nothing.

The remaining problem is a skill the piece cannot teach you. If the only thing standing between you and a clean performance is, say, pedaling you have never been shown, the piece cannot fix that. Go and learn the skill, then come back.

You are only still playing it because you have not chosen the next one. Very common, and easily solved: choose the next one.

One good reason to stay

If it is still improving, stay. A piece you can play from memory, musically, at tempo is worth far more than four pieces you half-know — and finishing something all the way through is a distinct experience that teaches you what “finished” feels like. If you have never had that with any piece, have it with this one.

Leaving is not losing it

The part adults do not expect: a piece keeps improving after you put it down. Set it aside for two months, come back, and it is often better than when you left — the awkward passages have loosened and the whole thing sits more easily under the hand.

So moving on is not abandonment. Put it in a short list of pieces to revisit, take something new, and open it again in the spring. It will feel like meeting someone you know well.

And keep the finished ones

Play the pieces you have finished, regularly, for no reason other than that you can. It is the warm-up on tired days, it is what you play when somebody asks, and it is the clearest evidence you have that the work is going somewhere.
