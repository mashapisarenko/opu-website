When Should You Start Using the Pedal? And How to Stop It Sounding Like Mud

The pedal is not a sustain button. It is a second instrument played with your foot, and it has its own technique — starting with the fact that the foot moves after the hand, never with it.
By Dr. Maria Pisarenko, DMA  ·  November 11, 2026

The pedal arrives in its proper place, not at the start, and the course is free: Open the free course →

The pedal is the most tempting thing on the instrument. Put your foot down and everything suddenly rings; the thin, careful sound of a beginner becomes warm and connected, and for about thirty seconds it feels like you have jumped a year ahead.

Then it turns to mud, and you cannot work out why, and the honest answer nobody gives is that the pedal is not a sustain button. It is a second instrument played with your foot, and it has its own technique.

What the pedal actually does

The one on the right — the only one that matters for a long time — lifts the dampers off every string in the piano at once. While it is down, every note you play keeps sounding, and so does every note you played before it, until you let the foot up.

That is the whole mechanism, and it explains both effects. Held down through one harmony, it connects and enriches. Held down through a change of harmony, it stacks the old notes on top of the new ones, and what you hear is not richness but everything at once.

The middle pedal and the left pedal are not your problem yet. The left softens, the middle does something different on nearly every instrument, and neither is needed in your first year.

When to start

After you can play the passage cleanly without it. That is the whole rule, and it is worth being strict about, because the pedal is very good at hiding things.

A pedal covers gaps between notes. That means it covers a hand that is not yet connecting notes properly — and connecting notes with the hand, letting one key up exactly as the next goes down, is a real skill that takes real time. If you pedal before you have it, you never build it, because the problem it solves is no longer audible to you. Adults who learn this way tend to discover a year later that nothing they play sounds like anything without the foot down, which is an unpleasant thing to find out.

So: learn the piece with the hands. Get the notes joined by the fingers. Then add the pedal, and it will make good playing better instead of making rough playing plausible.

In practice this puts the pedal somewhere in the second half of a first year for most adults — well after both hands are working together, and specifically once you are playing pieces built on chords that change.

The one movement that matters

Almost everyone's first instinct is to put the foot down at the same moment as the hand. It is the natural thing to do and it produces exactly the mud described above, because at the instant you press, the previous harmony is still sounding and you have just trapped it.

The technique is the opposite, and it has a name: overlapping pedaling, sometimes called syncopated pedaling.

Play the new note or chord first. Then, a fraction after, lift the foot and put it straight back down.

Up-down, immediately after the hand — not with it. The lift clears everything that was ringing; the new chord, already sounding, is caught by the foot coming back down. The join is seamless and nothing from the old harmony survives into the new one.

It is a coordination that feels wrong for a week, exactly like hands together did, and then becomes automatic. The order to hold in your head is simply: hand, then foot.

Where the foot goes, and how it moves

The heel stays on the floor. The whole motion is the ankle, and the ball of the foot never leaves the pedal. Lifting the entire leg is tiring, imprecise, and produces an audible clunk.

Let the pedal come all the way up when you clear it. A foot that only half releases leaves the dampers brushing the strings — the harmony does not properly clear, and you get a gray blur that is hard to diagnose because it is not obviously wrong, just never clean.

Do not let the mechanism make noise. A pedal dropped hard thumps, and the thump is in time with the music, so listeners hear it as part of the piece.

The only instruction that ultimately counts

Change the pedal when the harmony changes, and let your ear be the judge, not the page.

Printed pedal markings are a guide, they are often sparse, and they are written for a particular instrument in a particular room. Your piano, your room and your playing are not that. A room with hard floors and no curtains will blur at half the pedal a carpeted room tolerates.

So the working rule is the one every experienced player actually uses: the ear leads the foot. If it sounds thick, you have held it too long. Clear it and try again. This is a listening skill, and it develops faster than you would think once you know that listening is the job.

Three habits that cause nearly all pedal trouble

Holding it down continuously. Usually not a decision — the foot goes down at the start and is simply forgotten. If your playing sounds foggy and you cannot find the cause, check whether your foot has moved in the last minute.

Pedaling with the hand instead of after it. The default instinct, and the commonest single fault.

Pedaling a melody that moves by step. The pedal connects, but stepwise notes in one hand blur into each other badly. Long, slow, moving lines often want no pedal at all, and a piece that sounds cluttered frequently just needs the foot to stay up for eight bars.

A five-minute exercise

Play one chord. Put the foot down after it and listen to it ring until it dies. Then play a different chord and practice the change on its own: hand, then foot up-down, and listen for whether any of the first chord survived.

Four chords, twenty times, no music. It is boring, it takes five minutes, and it is the entire technique.

The part you cannot hear

This is the skill where self-assessment fails hardest, and it is not a failure of attention. While you are playing you are attending to your hands, and the blur produced by a foot half a beat late is precisely the kind of thing your ear smooths over in the moment. On a recording it is obvious instantly.

So record two minutes of something you pedal, with your phone placed so it picks up the sound honestly rather than close to the strings, and send it. The written feedback is free, and pedaling is one of the things it is most useful for — because the gap between what you hear while playing and what is actually in the room is at its widest here.
