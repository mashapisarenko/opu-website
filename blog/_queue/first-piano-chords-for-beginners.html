First Piano Chords for Beginners: The Three to Learn First

Three chords, C, F and G, will accompany a surprising amount of music. Here is how to find them, shape your hand for them, and practice them in five minutes a day.
By Dr. Maria Pisarenko, DMA  ·  January 31, 2027

Forty-one lessons in order, free, at your own pace: Start at Lesson 1 →

The first piano chords for a beginner to learn are C major, F major and G major, played in root position. In the key of C they are the I, IV and V chords, and together they will accompany a surprising amount of music, including three public-domain songs you probably already know: Amazing Grace, When the Saints Go Marching In and Jingle Bells.

Many adults think of chords as something advanced, reserved for people who play by ear or play in bands. They are not. A chord is simply three notes sounded together, and the three in this article can be found by landmarks, without counting from the bottom of the keyboard. If you have never read a note, you can still begin today. Nothing here needs a theory degree, only a keyboard, a few minutes a day, and a willingness to listen to what you play. I will keep the vocabulary plain and explain each word the first time it appears.

What a triad is

The chords here are triads: three notes, built by stacking every other letter. Start on a note, called the root, skip a letter and take the next, which is the third, then skip another and take the next, which is the fifth. From C that gives C, E and G. A major triad has a particular sound because of the distances: from the root to the third is four half steps, and from the third to the fifth is three. A half step is the shortest distance between two keys, and a whole step is two half steps, which the free course explains in Lesson 30 (see the lesson list by book page).

Root position means the root is the lowest of the three notes. That is the simplest way to see a chord and the one to learn first.

Why these three

Line up the C major scale: C, D, E, F, G, A, B. A chord built on the first note is called I, on the fourth note IV, and on the fifth note V. In C those are C, F and G, and they are called the primary chords. Put the three triads side by side, C-E-G, F-A-C and G-B-D, and you will find that between them they contain every note of the C major scale. That is why so many simple tunes in C sit comfortably on these three chords and little else. The same three chords, in a particular order, make the twelve-bar blues, which the free course reaches in Lesson 32.

Find each chord with landmarks

You do not need to count keys. You need two landmarks, which are in how to find notes on the piano: C is the white key immediately to the left of every group of two black keys, and F is the white key immediately to the left of every group of three black keys.

C major: C, E, G. Find any C by the two black keys. Play C, skip D, play E, skip F, play G. The chord is every other white key, three of them.

F major: F, A, C. Find F at the left of a group of three black keys. Play F, skip G, play A, skip B, play the next C.

G major: G, B, D. Within the group of three black keys, the white key between the first and second black keys is G. Play G, skip A, play B, skip C, play D. Or simply find F, and G is the next white key to the right.

The free course teaches these in context: the C major chord in Lesson 11 and the F major chord in Lesson 15. The G chord arrives there as G7 in Lesson 12, with a simplified three-note version, and Lesson 32 ties C, F and G7 together as the primary chords in C major. This article stays with the plain G major triad.

The hand shape

The hand shape for a chord is the same one as for a single note: fingers gently curved, as if you were holding a small apple, playing on the fingertips, with the wrist level with the hand. Piano hand position: why curved fingers matter explains why. For a triad in root position, the right hand normally uses the thumb, the middle finger and the little finger, which are fingers 1, 3 and 5, and the left hand uses 5, 3 and 1. The thumb and the little finger are the outer notes of the chord, and the middle finger sits between them.

Press all three notes at the same moment, and listen for whether they sound together. Chords played evenly sound like one note with three colors. If one note speaks before the others, the weaker finger needs more attention, not more force.

A five-minute daily drill

Five minutes is enough if you use them in order.

Minute one: set the hand. Rub your palms warm, make a fist and snap the fingers open, five times. Sit at the middle of the keyboard with your forearm level with the keys.

Minutes two and three: find and name. Right hand alone, find C, F and G in turn, press each triad, and say the name of each note out loud as you press it: C, E, G. Then the left hand alone.

Minutes four and five: change. Play C, then F, then G, then C again, four beats on each, counting one, two, three, four out loud. Slowly enough that the change is easy, not just possible. During the last beat of each chord, look at the place your hand is going next and let it travel there as one shape. Chord changes feel impossible when the hand is rebuilt note by note at every move.

Do this daily for a week and then add the left hand, one chord per bar, under a melody you already play.

Three problems you will meet

One note louder than the others. It is almost always the thumb or the little finger, and it is usually a sign of pushing rather than weight. Play the chord again, slowly, thinking of the weight of the arm falling evenly into all three fingertips.

A wrist that drops. Reaching for the fifth finger pulls the wrist down. Keep the wrist level with the hand, and if it collapses, stop and reset the shape rather than pushing through. Playing should never hurt.

Looking at your hands the whole time. At first you must look. Then practice finding each chord with one look, and eventually with none, by feeling where the black keys are. The black keys are your map in the dark.

Songs that use only these three chords

In the key of C, many simple songs can be accompanied with just C, F and G. Three public-domain examples:

  • Amazing Grace can be played with only C, F and G, though fuller arrangements add more chords.
  • When the Saints Go Marching In is a classic C, F and G piece, with a G7 for the G chord in many versions.
  • Jingle Bells, in its chorus, moves between the same three chords.

All three are in the free course, in Lessons 40, 15 and 10 (see the lesson list by book page), where you also meet the melodies. For the melodies themselves, see easy piano songs for adult beginners.

Where to go next

When the three chords feel easy, play them as you would a pattern, not as three separate shapes: the same relation between the three in any key. Questions adults ask

What are the first piano chords to learn?

C major, F major and G major, in root position. In the key of C they are the I, IV and V chords, and together they accompany a surprising amount of simple music.

What is a triad on the piano?

A triad is three notes built by stacking every other letter: a root, a third and a fifth. From C that gives C, E and G. In root position the root is the lowest of the three.

Which songs can I play with only C, F and G?

Amazing Grace, When the Saints Go Marching In and Jingle Bells can all be accompanied with just these three chords in the key of C. Some versions use G7 in place of the plain G chord, and fuller arrangements add more chords.
