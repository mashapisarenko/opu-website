Scriabin's Two Poems Op. 32: Color, Harmony, Pedal

Scriabin's Two Poems Op. 32 are short pieces where harmony becomes color. Here is what to listen for, and what pedal and touch have to do with that sound.
By Dr. Maria Pisarenko, DMA  ·  November 29, 2026

Taught by Dr. Maria Pisarenko, DMA — the complete beginner course is free: Start at Lesson 1 →

Alexander Scriabin was a Russian composer and pianist whose music sounds like no one else’s. His Two Poems, Op. 32, are short, but they contain a whole world of sound. I have performed both, and they are among the pieces I most enjoy explaining, because they show how harmony can work almost like color on a canvas.

Scriabin and color

Scriabin was fascinated by the connection between sound and color, and he associated musical keys with particular colors. Whatever one thinks of that idea, it is a useful way to listen to his music. Instead of following a melody and its accompaniment in the usual way, try listening to how the harmonies shift, like light changing in a room.

Poem No. 1 in F-sharp major

The first Poem is slow and dreamlike, marked Andante cantabile. It is one of Scriabin’s most loved pieces. The melody floats, and the rhythm often feels suspended, because Scriabin places notes against the beat in ways that blur the sense of a regular pulse. The harmonies are rich and full of added tones, so that the chords seem to glow rather than simply resolve.

Listen for the moments where the melody seems to hang in the air before moving on. Listen, too, for how the bass anchors the harmony from below while the upper voices shimmer. The piece grows to a warm climax and then returns to its original quiet state, as if waking gently from a dream.

Poem No. 2 in D major

The second Poem is completely different. It is marked Allegro, con eleganza, con fiducia: fast, with elegance, with confidence. It is assertive, built on strong rhythms and bold gestures. Where the first Poem dissolves, the second declares. Listen for the energy of its rhythmic figures and for the sense of forward drive. It is short and ends with conviction.

Played together, the two pieces form a pair: one inward and searching, the other outward and certain.

The role of the pedal

In Scriabin, the pedal is not an afterthought. It is part of the sound. The rich harmonies need resonance; without pedal, the chords can sound dry and broken. But too much pedal turns the music into a blur where nothing is clear.

A pianist playing these pieces changes the pedal often and listens closely each time. Sometimes the pedal is lifted only partly, so that the high notes clear while some of the bass resonance remains. The goal is a sound that is full but still transparent, like colored glass rather than fog.

Touch and balance

Because the chords are full of notes, balance inside each chord matters very much. The melody must sing above the harmony, and the inner voices must be soft enough to color the sound without covering the line. Scriabin was himself a pianist known for a delicate, refined touch, and his music invites that kind of listening.

For the first Poem in particular, the sound must be soft but never weak. A soft sound with depth comes from a relaxed arm and a slow, close contact with the key, not from barely touching it.

What you can try this week

  • Listen to the first Poem and notice how often you lose track of the beat. That is intentional. Let it carry you instead of counting.
  • Listen to the second Poem immediately afterward and describe the difference in one word for each piece.
  • At your piano, play a simple chord slowly with the pedal down and listen to it ring. Then play the same chord with a slightly different note added. Listen to how the color changes.
  • Practice changing the pedal: play a chord, then a second chord, and lift the pedal just as you play the second chord, putting it down again immediately after. Listen for a clean change with no gap and no blur.
  • Try a single melody note very softly over a held chord. Can you make the top note sing while the rest stays quiet?

Scriabin teaches us to listen to sound itself: not just which notes are played, but how they ring together. That kind of listening will improve anything you play.
