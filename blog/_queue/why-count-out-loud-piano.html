Counting Out Loud Feels Childish. Do It Anyway.

Nobody hears you. And the thing counting fixes is the thing a listener notices first — long before they notice a wrong note.
By Dr. Maria Pisarenko, DMA  ·  November 13, 2026

The counting is taught in order, from the first lesson, and the course is free: Open the free course →

I can usually tell within thirty seconds of hearing a recording whether an adult has been counting out loud. Not from the rhythm being wrong in an obvious way — from something subtler that is very hard to describe and very easy to hear: the playing has no floor under it. Notes arrive roughly when they should, the piece is recognizable, and nothing is holding it steady.

Counting out loud is the floor. It is also the first instruction adults quietly drop, usually inside the first week, and always for the same reason: it feels ridiculous to sit alone in your own house saying one two three four at a piano.

So let me make the case properly, because this is the cheapest large improvement available to you and almost nobody takes it.

Rhythm is what a listener hears first

Play a familiar tune with one wrong note and steady time, and most listeners will not notice the wrong note. Play it with every note correct and uneven time, and everyone notices, including people who have never touched an instrument. They will not say your rhythm was uneven — they will say it sounded like someone learning. That impression comes almost entirely from time.

This ordering surprises adults, because wrong notes feel like the embarrassing mistakes. They are not. A wrong note is an event that passes. Unsteady time is a property of the whole performance.

What counting actually does that thinking the beat does not

Nearly everybody who has stopped counting will tell you they are still counting in their head. I believe them. The trouble is that counting in your head is elastic: it stretches at the hard bar and contracts at the easy one, and it does so without your noticing, because the same attention that is supposed to be keeping time is busy finding a note.

Out loud, it cannot stretch. Your voice is a physical thing with its own steady pulse, and it is not doing the playing, so it is not disturbed by the playing. That is the whole mechanism. You are giving your hands an external reference that does not flinch when they do.

It also makes one very specific fault impossible to hide. Almost every self-taught adult speeds up through the passages they find easy and slows through the ones they find hard — which is precisely backwards, and which is invisible from the inside. Out loud, you hear yourself do it in the moment.

How to count so that it helps

Count the beats of the bar, not the notes. In four-four: one two three four, over and over, regardless of how many notes fall inside each beat. The count belongs to the bar, not to your hands.

Count before you play, and keep counting after you stop. Two bars of counting alone before the first note, so the pulse exists before the music does. This is the single change that fixes a shaky opening, which is where most recordings wobble.

Count at the speed you can actually play. If the count has to wait for your hands, the count is too fast — which means the piece is too fast. This is the most useful diagnostic in the whole method: your voice tells you your real tempo, and it is almost always slower than the one you have been attempting.

Say the held notes through. A half note is not one and then silence in your head. It is one, two, with the key still down on two. Adults cut long notes short more often than they play them long, and counting through the value is the fix.

Keep the voice level. If you get louder when the music gets busier, the count is following the music instead of leading it.

When you can stop

You do not count out loud forever, and you do not count out loud in a performance. You count out loud while a passage is being learned, and you stop when the passage no longer needs it — which you can test: play it without counting, record it, and see whether the time held. If it did, that passage has internalized the pulse. If it did not, it had been leaning on the count, which is exactly what the count is for.

Expect to come back to it. A new piece with an unfamiliar rhythm needs counting again, and returning to it is not a regression.

A note about the metronome, since everyone asks

A metronome is a good tool later and a poor substitute now. It tells you that you were wrong; it does not tell you where the beat is. Counting builds the pulse inside you; a metronome tests it. Build first, test second — and if a metronome makes you tense, put it away for a few months. Nothing in your first year requires one.

The reason this matters more for you than for a child

A child in a lesson counts out loud because the teacher counts with them, every week, for years, and nobody asks whether it feels silly. Adults teaching themselves have no one in the room, and the instruction arrives as a line in a book that is easy to skip.

Which means the habit that most reliably makes playing sound like music is also the one most reliably dropped — not because adults are less disciplined, but because there is nobody there to keep it honest.

The part you cannot hear

Time is the hardest thing to judge in your own playing, because you are inside it. The rushing that is obvious to a listener is smoothed over by your own expectations of what you meant to play.

So record two minutes — counting out loud, on purpose, with the phone at the side of the keyboard — and send it. The written feedback is free, and time is one of the things it is most useful for, because the gap between what you felt and what actually happened is widest here.
