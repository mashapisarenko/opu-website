Do I Need an Audio Interface for a Digital Piano?

Most adults do not need one. Here is how to tell, and which three I would look at if you do.
By Dr. Maria Pisarenko, DMA  ·  January 30, 2027

Shopping for this? My picks, chosen by a concert pianist: Audio Interfaces and Microphones and Interfaces.

You do not need an audio interface to play a digital piano, to hear it through headphones, or to use it with most piano apps. You need one when you want to record the piano's sound with good quality, or to add a microphone, and your piano cannot send audio over its own USB cable. If you only want to practice, stop here and save your money.

How I choose

The question is not which interface is best. It is whether you need one at all, so I start with what you want to do, and only then with the box. I have not played every instrument or used every device in this guide, and I do not say that I have. What I give you is what I look for as a concert pianist who teaches adults, checked against what each Amazon listing and manufacturer page actually states.

What an audio interface actually does

An audio interface is a small box that turns sound into a signal your computer can record, and turns the computer's signal back into sound for your headphones. It has inputs for microphones and instruments, a headphone output, and a USB cable to the computer. A digital piano already makes its own sound, so for a piano with a line output or a headphone jack, an interface is the clean way to get that sound into a recording program.

When you do not need one

  • You only practice. Headphones plugged into the piano are enough.
  • You use a piano app or notation software. USB carries MIDI, which tells the software which notes you played. Many pianos have a computer USB port; Roland lists one on the FP-10, and Casio lists USB Type A and Type B ports on the CDP-S360. See how to connect a digital piano to a computer.
  • Your piano sends audio over USB. Yamaha lists a 24-bit stereo USB audio interface on the P-225, which means a single cable can carry the piano's sound into a recording program. Check your manual for the same feature on your model.

When you do

  • You want to record the sound, and your piano has no USB audio. Run a cable from the piano's line output into an interface.
  • You want to record a microphone, for your voice or for an acoustic piano. A microphone needs a preamp, and an interface has one.
  • You want to record a second instrument or a stereo pair of microphones. You then need two microphone inputs. Read the best audio interface for beginners.

What I would look at, in order

First, the number of microphone preamps: one is enough for a digital piano's line output and a voice; two are needed for a stereo pair. Second, whether the headphone output is clear and quiet, because you will listen to your own playing on it. Third, whether it runs from the computer's USB cable, so there is no power brick. After those, I do not care about the feature list. A student who finds the box simple will record more often, and recording yourself is how you hear what your teacher would hear.

Focusrite Scarlett Solo 4th Gen

Who it is for: A pianist who records one microphone, or the stereo line from a digital piano, and wants a simple box.

What the listing says: Focusrite lists one microphone preamp, one instrument input and one line input, two balanced line outputs, one headphone output, 24-bit/192 kHz converters, phantom power, loopback, Air mode, USB-C bus power, and software including Ableton Live Lite, Pro Tools Intro+ and Cubase LE. Its spec page does not list MIDI.

What I look for: A clean, quiet recording of a piano starts with a quiet preamp and an honest headphone output. I look at how simple the front panel is: the fewer decisions between the piano and the file, the more often a student will actually record.

The simplest answer for most adults, and the one I would suggest for a digital piano's line output.

Audient EVO 4

Who it is for: A beginner who wants two microphone inputs, so a stereo pair of microphones fits, in a small bus-powered box.

What the listing says: Audient lists two microphone preamps, two inputs of which one is a JFET instrument input, two outputs, a headphone output, SmartGain, loopback, USB bus power and a USB-C connection. Its tech-spec page lists 48 V phantom power, a 360 g unit and a 1 m USB-C to USB-A cable in the box. I did not find MIDI on the pages I read.

What I look for: A layout I can read at a glance, and two microphone preamps. Two preamps are what let me recommend a stereo pair of microphones on a piano, which a one-preamp box cannot do.

The one to buy if you think you will record an acoustic piano with two microphones one day.

Pros
  • Two microphone preamps, so a stereo pair fits
  • SmartGain sets the input levels for you, which helps a beginner
Cons

  • I did not find MIDI on the pages I read

Buy it if you may want to record a piano with two microphones. Skip it if you will only ever record one microphone or one cable from a piano.

Questions adults ask

Do I need an audio interface to record my digital piano?

Not always. If your piano sends audio over USB you can record with one cable. If it does not, an interface lets you record its line output cleanly.

Do I need an audio interface for MIDI?

No. MIDI travels over a USB cable on most digital pianos, and the computer reads it directly.

Is a USB microphone a substitute?

For a voice, often yes. For a stereo recording of a piano, an interface with two preamps and two microphones gives you more control.
