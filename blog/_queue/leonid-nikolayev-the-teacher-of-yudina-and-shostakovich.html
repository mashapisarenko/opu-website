Leonid Nikolayev: The Teacher Behind Yudina and Shostakovich

Leonid Nikolayev taught Yudina, Sofronitsky and Shostakovich and wanted thinking musicians, not just pianists; this phrase shapes how I teach adult beginners to listen, reason and play.
By Dr. Maria Pisarenko, DMA  ·  October 27, 2026

Taught by Dr. Maria Pisarenko, DMA — the complete beginner course is free: Start at Lesson 1 →

Few piano teachers have a student list as striking as Leonid Nikolayev’s. Maria Yudina, Vladimir Sofronitsky and Dmitri Shostakovich all studied with him. Yet what interests me most is not the list but a single sentence about how he taught, because it changed the way I teach adults.

Who Leonid Nikolayev was

Leonid Nikolayev was a Russian pianist, composer and teacher. He was born in Kyiv and studied at the Moscow Conservatory with Sergei Taneyev and Mikhail Ippolitov-Ivanov. He taught for many years at the Conservatory in Petrograd, later called Leningrad, and for a time served as its director. He was named a People’s Artist, one of the highest honors for artists in the Soviet Union. He died in Tashkent, where he had been evacuated during the war.

A caution about names: Russian music has several people called Nikolaev or Tatiana Nikolayeva, and they are easy to confuse. Leonid Nikolayev, the Leningrad teacher, is not Tatiana Nikolayeva, the pianist, and he is not Alexander Alexandrovich Nikolaev, who wrote the School of Piano Playing. They are three different people, and I draw on all three in different ways.

The sentence

Dmitri Shostakovich dedicated his Piano Sonata No. 2 to his former teacher, and he said that Leonid Nikolayev trained not simply pianists, but in the first place thinking musicians. Consider what a composer of that stature is saying. He is not praising technique or virtuosity first. He is praising a habit of mind.

What a “thinking musician” is

I will not put words in Leonid Nikolayev’s mouth, since I never met him and will not invent his lessons. What I can offer is what the phrase means to me, having inherited it through my own teachers. A thinking musician:

  • hears the music in the head before the hands move;
  • knows the shape of the whole piece, not only the measure in front of her;
  • can say why a phrase rises, where it rests, and what the harmony is doing;
  • makes choices on purpose and can explain them;
  • listens to the sound she actually produces and adjusts it.

Notice that none of this is reserved for prodigies. Every item on that list can be practiced by an adult on a digital piano in a spare room.

How the idea reaches me

At the Gnessin Russian Academy of Music, my teacher Yuri Vladimirovich Ponizovkin was a pupil of Maria Yudina, who studied with Leonid Nikolayev. That is one of the two branches of my teaching lineage. I do not claim to reproduce his lessons, which would be absurd. I claim only to have received the same expectation: the pianist is a musician first, and a musician thinks.

Why this shapes how I teach adults

Adults often arrive apologizing. They are sure they are too old, too busy, too late. And they assume the answer is to push their fingers harder. I take the opposite view. Adults already have something children lack: a mature mind that can reason, compare and remember. A teacher in the tradition of the thinking musician puts that mind to work.

  • See it, hear it, then play it. I ask students to look at a phrase, sing or imagine it, and only then touch the keys.
  • Ask why. Why does this passage sound tense? Why does the left hand change here? An answer you reasoned out sticks better than a fingering you were told.
  • Judge by sound. Rather than watching your hands, listen. The sound is the truth.
  • Let exercises grow out of the piece. If a passage is hard, we build a small exercise from that very passage, so that the work always serves the music.

Try it this week

Pick a short piece you are working on. Before you play it again, do three things. Say aloud, in your own words, what happens in the piece from beginning to end. Hum the opening phrase without the piano. Then play it and ask whether the sound matched what you imagined. That small ritual is the thinking musician in action, and it will improve your playing faster than another ten repetitions.

A good teacher does not make you dependent. The aim of a thinking musician’s education is that, in time, you do not need me to tell you what is wrong, because you can hear it yourself.
