The Gnessin Academy and the Teachers Who Shaped Me

Dr. Pisarenko describes the Gnessin Russian Academy of Music, her teachers and masterclasses, and explains why collaborative piano trains listening and lets adults make music with others.
By Dr. Maria Pisarenko, DMA  ·  October 25, 2026

Taught by Dr. Maria Pisarenko, DMA — the complete beginner course is free: Start at Lesson 1 →

Much of what I know about music, I learned in Moscow at the Gnessin Russian Academy of Music. When I mention it to American students, most have never heard of it, so let me tell you what it is and why it matters to anyone learning piano as an adult.

What the Gnessin Academy is

The Gnessin Russian Academy of Music, often spelled Gnesin, is a music institution in Moscow. It grew out of a private music school founded by the Gnesin sisters, and over the decades it developed into one of Russia’s major centers of musical education. Its history is long and has gone through many reorganizations, but the idea at its core has stayed constant: serious, thorough training of performers and teachers side by side.

I earned my degrees there in Piano Performance, Pedagogy and Collaborative Piano. I will say more about that last one shortly, because it changed the way I listen.

The teachers who shaped me

My teacher at Gnessin was Yuri Vladimirovich Ponizovkin, a pupil of Maria Yudina and a scholar of Rachmaninoff. Through him I belong to a line that leads back to Leonid Nikolayev, the Leningrad teacher whose students included Yudina and Dmitri Shostakovich. Shostakovich dedicated his Second Piano Sonata to Leonid Nikolayev, and he said that Leonid Nikolayev trained not simply pianists but, in the first place, thinking musicians. I think about that phrase often when I teach. A thinking musician is exactly what an adult learner wants to become.

At Gnessin I also learned from Dr. Lina Bulatova and Dr. Vera Nossina, who each added something to my understanding that I could not have found alone. A good teacher does not just correct your mistakes. A good teacher changes what you notice.

Learning from master teachers

As a student I also worked, in lessons and masterclasses, with a number of remarkable musicians: Eliso Virsaladze, Vera Gornostayeva, Sergei Dorensky, Lev Naumov and Vladimir Krainev. I also played for Tatiana Nikolayeva herself. Each of these musicians approached the piano differently, and that was the lesson. There is no single correct way to shape a phrase, only a way that is convincing, honest and listened to.

If you are teaching yourself, take this to heart: do not search for the one perfect authority. Listen widely, compare, and train your own judgment. That is what these encounters gave me.

Why collaborative piano made me a better listener

Collaborative piano is the art of accompanying: playing with a singer, a violinist, a cellist, a choir. Many pianists treat it as secondary. I came to see it as one of the best schools of listening there is.

When you accompany, you cannot simply play your own part well. You must hear the other musician breathe. You must follow a phrase that is not yours, adjust your weight so you support rather than cover, and sense when a singer needs a little more time. Every one of those skills is a listening skill, and every one of them improves your solo playing too.

It also teaches humility in the best sense. The music is bigger than your part.

What this means for you

Here is the practical point for adults. You do not have to wait until you are “good enough” to play with others. Playing with someone else is not a reward for finishing your training. It is part of the training.

  • Play simple music with a friend. A melody on a recorder, a violin, a voice or a guitar, with you providing a slow, steady harmony, is a genuine musical act.
  • Sing a familiar song and accompany yourself. You will quickly discover how much listening is required.
  • Play along with recordings, quietly. Notice where you rush, where you lag, and where the music breathes.
  • Listen to the other part before your own. In any duet, the question is not “Am I correct?” but “Are we together?”

An accompanist learns to be useful to another person’s music. That generosity is a wonderful habit to bring to the keyboard, and adults are often better at it than they expect.

A lineage is a way of listening

I mention my teachers not as decoration, but because they handed me ways of hearing. Those ways of hearing can be passed to you in plain, practical form: listen first, support the line, let the sound lead the hands. You do not need a Moscow education to use them. You need only a piano, some attention, and the willingness to play with an open ear.
