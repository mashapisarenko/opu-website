Beethoven's Choral Fantasy: Piano, Choir and Orchestra

Beethoven's Choral Fantasy starts with the piano alone, then brings in the orchestra and finally a choir. Here is how to follow it from first note to last.
By Dr. Maria Pisarenko, DMA  ·  December 6, 2026

Taught by Dr. Maria Pisarenko, DMA — the complete beginner course is free: Start at Lesson 1 →

Beethoven’s Choral Fantasy, Op. 80, is one of the most unusual works he ever wrote. It is not a concerto, not a symphony and not a choral piece in the usual sense. It combines all three. I have performed it as soloist, and it is one of the most joyful experiences a pianist can have on stage, because it begins with the piano completely alone and ends with everyone on stage singing and playing together.

How it came to be

Beethoven wrote the Choral Fantasy as the finale of a famous long concert in Vienna in 1808, a concert at which his Fifth and Sixth Symphonies were also first heard. He wanted a piece that would bring together all the performers of the evening. Beethoven himself played the piano part, and he improvised the opening solo at that concert. The written introduction we play today was composed afterward.

The piano introduction

The work begins with a long solo for piano, marked Adagio. It has the character of an improvisation: grand chords, sweeping runs and quieter, searching passages. There is no steady theme yet. It feels as if Beethoven is gathering his thoughts in front of the audience.

For the pianist, this opening is a serious test. The whole hall is waiting, the orchestra and choir are sitting silently and the soloist must hold everyone’s attention alone. The music needs a sense of freedom, as though it is being invented in the moment, but also a clear direction, so that the listener feels it is going somewhere.

The entrance of the orchestra

After the introduction, the orchestra enters quietly, beginning with the low strings, and the piano and orchestra begin a kind of conversation. Then the piano introduces the main theme, a simple, folk-like tune. Beethoven took it from an earlier song of his own.

Variations

The theme is then passed around the orchestra in a series of variations. Listen for the instruments that take it up in turn: the flute, then the oboes, then the clarinets with the bassoon and then a small group of solo strings. Finally, the full orchestra plays it. The piano surrounds these variations with decorative figures and returns to lead the music through several changes of mood, including a dramatic minor-key section and a march-like passage.

This is where the pianist must listen most carefully, because the role changes all the time: soloist, accompanist, partner in a dialogue with the orchestra.

The choir

Near the end, after a quieter passage, the solo singers enter, followed by the full choir. They sing a text in praise of music and harmony. The theme that the orchestra played is now sung, and the work builds to a bright, triumphant ending, with piano, orchestra and choir together.

Many listeners notice that the main theme sounds like an early relative of the “Ode to Joy” melody that Beethoven later used in the finale of his Ninth Symphony. The resemblance is clear, and so is the idea: music bringing people together.

What this asks of the pianist

  • Command alone. The introduction must be confident and shaped, not hesitant.
  • Flexibility. The pianist must move constantly between leading and supporting.
  • Balance. With a full orchestra and choir, the piano must find moments to be heard and moments to step back.
  • Joy. This piece is a celebration. If the pianist plays it carefully but without warmth, it misses its point.

What you can try this week

  • Listen to the piano introduction alone and notice where the music seems to search and where it seems certain.
  • When the main theme appears, follow it through the variations. Name each instrument you hear taking the tune.
  • Listen for the moment the choir enters and how the whole texture opens up.
  • At your piano, take a simple melody and play it in three ways: softly as a solo, then with a quiet left-hand accompaniment and then with fuller chords. Notice how the same tune changes character.
  • Try improvising for one minute on just a few chords you know. Do not worry about mistakes. Beethoven began this piece by improvising, and that freedom is part of music too.

The Choral Fantasy begins with one person at a piano and ends with a hall full of sound. It is a good picture of what music can do.
