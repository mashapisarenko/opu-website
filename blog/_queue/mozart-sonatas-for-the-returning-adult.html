Mozart Sonatas for the Returning Adult

Mozart's sonatas look simple and expose every detail, so a returning adult should choose movements carefully and practice them for clarity, evenness, and singing tone.
By Dr. Maria Pisarenko, DMA  ·  January 19, 2027

Taught by Dr. Maria Pisarenko, DMA — the complete beginner course is free: Start at Lesson 1 →

Many adults who return to the piano have a Mozart sonata somewhere in their memory: a first movement they played as teenagers, or a piece they always meant to learn. Mozart is a natural place to come back to. The notes look clean on the page, the textures are thin, and the music is endlessly beautiful.

It is also, as almost every pianist will tell you, much harder than it looks. The pianist Artur Schnabel put it famously: Mozart’s sonatas are too easy for children and too difficult for artists. Teachers often turn that around for adult students, and the version I hear most is “too easy for children, too hard for adults.” Both versions say the same thing. A child can play the notes. Playing them well takes everything you have.

Why Mozart is so exposing

In Romantic music, a thick texture and the pedal can hide a great deal. Mozart hides nothing. A melody sits above a light accompaniment, often a single line in each hand. Every uneven note in a scale, every bump in an Alberti bass, every phrase that ends too loudly is heard at once.

For a returning adult, that is both the challenge and the value. Mozart teaches clarity, evenness, control of dynamics, and a singing tone more directly than almost any other composer. If you can make a Mozart slow movement sound simple, you have learned something that serves you in all other music.

Movements to consider

You do not have to learn whole sonatas. Single movements are complete pieces in themselves. Here are several I would suggest, in rough order of difficulty:

  • Sonata in C major, K. 545, second movement (Andante). A gentle melody over a steady accompaniment. The challenge is keeping the left hand quiet and even under a singing right hand.
  • Sonata in C major, K. 545, first movement. Often called the “easy” sonata, and the most familiar. The scales and broken chords are excellent training, but they must be completely even to sound right.
  • Sonata in G major, K. 283, first movement. Bright and graceful, with clear phrases and a light texture that suits hands still regaining agility.
  • Sonata in A major, K. 331, first movement (theme). The opening theme of this set of variations is lovely on its own and asks for a warm, lyrical touch. Some of the later variations are considerably harder.
  • Sonata in C major, K. 330, second movement (Andante cantabile). Slow, expressive, and intimate. A wonderful study in phrasing for someone ready for more subtle music.

The famous Rondo alla Turca from K. 331 is tempting, but it is fast, needs strong control of repeated figures and octaves, and is better left until your hands are secure.

How to practice Mozart alone

Mozart rewards a particular kind of patience. Here is what I recommend:

  • Practice hands separately longer than you think you need. Each hand must be clean on its own before you put them together.
  • Keep the accompaniment quiet. In an Alberti bass, let the lowest note have a little weight and keep the rest light. Play the left hand alone, very softly, until it feels easy.
  • Sing the melody. Literally, out loud. Then try to make the piano breathe in the same places your voice did.
  • Use the metronome to check, not to drive. Turn it on to find uneven spots in scales, then turn it off and play musically.
  • Be sparing with pedal. Many passages need none. When you use it, keep it short and listen for clarity.
  • Record and listen for evenness. Scales that feel smooth under the hand often sound bumpy on a recording.

What to expect

Expect Mozart to take longer than you planned. A movement that you can read in an evening may need weeks before it sounds natural. That is normal, and it is not a sign that you have chosen something too difficult. It is simply how Mozart works. Every pianist, at every level, continues to learn from these pieces.

The reward is real. When a Mozart phrase finally sounds effortless, you will have gained control and sensitivity that no exercise could have given you. Choose one slow movement from the list above, give it a month, and let it teach you.
