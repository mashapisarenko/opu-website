The Grieg Piano Concerto Opening: What It Asks

A drum roll, an orchestral chord, then a cascade from the top of the keyboard: here is what happens in the famous opening of Grieg's Piano Concerto and what it demands.
By Dr. Maria Pisarenko, DMA  ·  December 5, 2026

Taught by Dr. Maria Pisarenko, DMA — the complete beginner course is free: Start at Lesson 1 →

Few concertos begin as dramatically as Edvard Grieg’s Piano Concerto in A minor, Op. 16. Even listeners who know nothing else about Grieg tend to recognize its opening. I have performed this concerto with orchestra, and I want to describe what happens in those first moments, and what they ask of the pianist who has to deliver them.

What you hear

The concerto begins with a roll on the timpani that grows from very soft to loud. At the peak, the orchestra plays a chord, and the piano enters immediately with a powerful cascade of chords and octaves falling from high on the keyboard down to the bass. It is a gesture that announces both the soloist and the character of the whole work: bold, emotional and full of color.

The descending figure is built on a short falling pattern of notes. This pattern, a step down followed by a larger fall, is often associated with the folk music of Grieg’s native Norway, and it returns in different forms throughout the concerto. Listen for it later; once you know it, you will hear it again.

After the piano lands in the bass, the orchestra introduces the main theme, a gentle, songlike melody played first by the woodwinds. Then the piano takes it up. So the opening moves from drama to lyricism within a minute, and that contrast is the heart of the work.

What the opening asks of the pianist

The opening lasts only a few seconds, but it is one of the most exposed moments in the concerto repertoire.

  • Timing with the orchestra. The piano must enter exactly at the peak of the timpani roll and orchestral chord. The pianist and conductor need to agree on that moment, and the pianist must be ready before it arrives.
  • Power without harshness. The cascade is loud, but it must sound full and ringing, not banged. That requires the weight of a relaxed arm moving down the keyboard, not stiff fingers striking from above.
  • Clarity in the fall. Each chord in the descent should be heard. If the pianist rushes, the cascade becomes a smear. If they slow down too much, it loses its sweep.
  • Arriving in the bass. The descent ends in the lowest register of the piano, and the landing must feel like an arrival, solid and resonant.
  • Calm before it begins. The pianist sits in silence while the drum roll grows. Being physically relaxed and mentally ready at that moment is part of the performance.

The rest of the first movement

After the opening, the first movement alternates between lyrical melodies and energetic passages. There is a second theme, introduced by the cellos and then taken up by the piano, that is tender and warm. Near the end of the movement, the piano plays a long cadenza, a solo passage without orchestra, which Grieg wrote out in full. It builds to an enormous climax before the orchestra returns.

Listening to the whole movement, notice how often the drama of the opening and the gentleness of the main theme trade places. That is the emotional pattern of the concerto.

What this means for an adult at the piano

You may not be preparing a concerto, but the opening of the Grieg teaches something useful. A dramatic, loud passage depends on preparation: knowing where the hands are going before they move, staying relaxed and letting weight do the work. These ideas apply just as much to a loud chord in a beginner piece.

What you can try this week

  • Listen to the opening several times. Count how many chords you can hear in the piano’s descent.
  • Listen past the opening until the main theme in the woodwinds, and notice the change of mood.
  • At the piano, play a simple chord high on the keyboard, then the same chord an octave lower, then lower again, letting your relaxed arm move the hand. Listen for a full, ringing sound on each.
  • Before you play the first note of any piece, sit in silence for a few seconds and feel ready. Notice whether the first sound is better.

The Grieg opening seems to explode out of nowhere, but in truth it comes out of stillness and preparation. That is a lesson for any first note you play.
