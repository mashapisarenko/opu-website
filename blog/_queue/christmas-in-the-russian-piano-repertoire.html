Christmas in Russian Piano Music: Winter at the Keyboard

Russian Christmas falls on January 7, and Russian composers wrote winter music that is intimate and songful; here is a guide to the best-known pieces.
By Dr. Maria Pisarenko, DMA  ·  December 20, 2026

Taught by Dr. Maria Pisarenko, DMA — the complete beginner course is free: Start at Lesson 1 →

I was trained in Moscow, at the Central Music School of the Moscow Tchaikovsky Conservatory and later at the Gnessin Academy, and the Russian piano repertoire is my musical home language. Its winter music is a rich subject, because in Russia winter is not a background. It is a season with its own sounds, customs and stories, and the composers wrote them down.

Christmas on January 7

First, a calendar note that surprises many people. The Russian Orthodox Church follows the older Julian calendar for its feasts, so Christmas in Russia falls on January 7, not December 25. The period that follows, until the feast of Epiphany, is called Svyatki, the holy days of Christmastide. Traditionally it was a time of carol singing, visiting, dressing up and, among young people, playful fortune-telling about the future.

During the Soviet era, Christmas was not officially celebrated, and New Year became the great winter holiday, with the decorated tree, gifts and family gathering moving to the night of December 31. Today many families celebrate both, so in Russia the holiday season stretches from New Year’s Eve through Orthodox Christmas and beyond.

Tchaikovsky’s December

The best-known Christmas piece in Russian piano music is “December” from Tchaikovsky’s The Seasons. Its Russian title is Svyatki, the same word for Christmastide. The epigraph, from the poet Vasily Zhukovsky, describes young women on Christmas Eve telling their fortunes. And the music is a waltz: graceful, intimate, a little wistful. It is the sound of a warm room on a winter night rather than church bells or a grand celebration.

More winter at the piano

Tchaikovsky gives us more than one winter page.

  • “January” from The Seasons. Its title is usually translated as “By the Fireside.” Quiet, reflective, a little dreamy, it is music for a long evening indoors.
  • “February” from The Seasons. Called “Carnival,” it depicts Maslenitsa, the lively festival at the end of winter. Bright and energetic, it is winter turning toward spring.
  • “Winter Morning” from the Album for the Young, Op. 39. A short, restless piece that many Russian pianists meet early in their study. It is one of the most accessible pieces on this list, and adults can enjoy it too.

Beyond the piano, Russian composers loved the winter story of Christmas Eve by Nikolai Gogol, in which a village blacksmith flies to the capital to fetch slippers for his beloved. Tchaikovsky set it as an opera, later revised as Cherevichki, and Rimsky-Korsakov wrote his own opera called Christmas Eve. Georgy Sviridov’s music for The Snowstorm, after Pushkin, includes a waltz that is often heard in piano arrangements.

What these pieces share

Listen to several of them, and you will notice a common mood. Russian winter music is rarely loud and festive in the way of a Western Christmas march. It tends to be intimate, songful and slightly melancholy even when it dances. There is warmth, but it is the warmth of a lamp against a dark window. Melody comes first. The pianist’s task is to make it sing, with a rounded tone and long phrases, never hard or percussive.

That is very much the heart of the Russian piano tradition I was raised in: the piano as a singing instrument, and the pianist as someone who shapes a line the way a singer does.

For an adult learner

Some of this music is within reach sooner than you might think. Tchaikovsky’s Album for the Young was written for young players, and several of its pieces are approachable after a year or two of steady work. The Seasons is harder, generally intermediate, but simplified arrangements of “December” and “January” exist and can be a lovely bridge. In any case, listening is a beginning. Knowing the mood of this music will change how you play every lyrical piece you learn.

This week at the piano

Listen to “December” and “January” from The Seasons one evening this week. Then sit at the piano and play any simple melody you know, as softly and as singingly as you can, letting each phrase end with a small breath. If you celebrate on January 7, or simply want to stretch the season, you have a few extra weeks to make it beautiful.
