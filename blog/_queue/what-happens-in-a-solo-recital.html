What Really Happens in a Solo Piano Recital

A solo recital is built from invisible decisions: programming, layered memory, the hall and the opening silence. Dr. Pisarenko turns them into advice for adults who play for family.
By Dr. Maria Pisarenko, DMA  ·  October 24, 2026

Taught by Dr. Maria Pisarenko, DMA — the complete beginner course is free: Start at Lesson 1 →

A recital looks simple from the audience: a pianist walks out, sits down and plays. But a solo recital is built from many invisible decisions, and understanding them can change the way you prepare to play even a few pieces for your family at home. I gave my first solo recital as a child, and I have played solo programs in Russia, Europe, Asia and the United States, including at the Royal Academy of Music in London, the Paris Conservatory, the Moscow Tchaikovsky Conservatory and Santa Cecilia in Rome. On my YouTube channel you can watch footage I labeled plainly: live and unedited, from my solo recital. No second takes, no patching. I want to tell you what is behind a performance like that.

Programming: a recital is a journey, not a list

The first decision is what to play and in what order. A program is a shape, not a pile of favorites. A pianist thinks about contrast (a quiet piece after a loud one), about pacing (where the audience can rest, where it needs energy), and about the first piece, which has to settle both the player and the hall. The final piece should feel like an arrival.

For you, this means something practical. If you are going to play for family, choose three short pieces in different moods rather than one long piece you are not sure of. Begin with something you can play well even when nervous.

Memory: more than the fingers

Many people think a recital is a test of memory, and for a pianist it partly is. But memorizing well does not mean repeating until the hands remember. A reliable memory has several layers: the ear remembers how the music sounds, the mind remembers its structure and harmony, the hands remember the movements. When one layer fails, another catches you.

If you only trust your fingers, one slip and the whole piece collapses. If you can hear the next phrase in your head before you play it, you can find your way back.

You do not have to memorize for family. Put the score on the stand and use it as a safety net. But practice the same way, listening ahead.

The hall: you play the room, not just the piano

Every hall answers differently. Some rooms are dry and show every detail; others are warm and blur fast passages. A pianist arrives early, tries the instrument and listens to how the sound travels, then adjusts: slower in a resonant space, with clearer articulation in a dry one, with more or less pedal.

At home the same principle applies. The piano in your living room will sound different when the room is full of people, because bodies and sofas absorb sound. Test it. Play a piece once before guests arrive, and again after they sit. You will probably notice you need a little more sound and a little more clarity.

The silence before the first chord

This is the part I love most. Before the first note, there is a pause that belongs to the performer and the listeners together. In that moment the audience settles, the player hears the opening inside, and the room agrees to listen. In my footage you can see it, and if you watch carefully you can hear the room go quiet. I will say more about this in a separate essay, because it deserves its own space.

What the unedited part teaches

I label my recital footage as unedited for a reason. Modern recordings are often assembled from many takes, which is useful, but it can leave the impression that real playing is flawless. It is not. A live performance has a pulse of its own, and small imperfections do not ruin it. What matters is that the music has a line, and that the player does not stop listening.

If you watch the footage, I suggest you ignore the question “did she make a mistake?” and ask instead “where is the music going?” This is how audiences actually listen, and it is a kinder way to listen to yourself.

Practical takeaways for playing for family

  • Choose three short pieces that you can play securely, in different moods.
  • Prepare the first four bars until they work even when your hands are cold.
  • Practice starting from several places in each piece, so a slip is not the end.
  • Play once for an empty room and once with people present, and notice the difference.
  • Pause before you begin. Let the room settle and hear the first phrase inside.
  • Keep going. A listener who loves you wants the music, not a perfect record.
  • Record yourself in one unedited take and listen once a week. It is the most honest teacher you will ever have.

A recital, in the end, is not a demonstration of power. It is an act of offering something you have prepared to people who are willing to listen. You can do that in a concert hall, and you can do that at your own kitchen table.
