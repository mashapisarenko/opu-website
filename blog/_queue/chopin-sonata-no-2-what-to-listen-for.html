Chopin's Funeral March Sonata: What to Listen For

Chopin's Second Sonata is famous for its funeral march, but the whole work tells one story. Here is what to listen for in each of its four movements.
By Dr. Maria Pisarenko, DMA  ·  November 21, 2026

Taught by Dr. Maria Pisarenko, DMA — the complete beginner course is free: Start at Lesson 1 →

Almost everyone knows the funeral march from Chopin’s Piano Sonata No. 2 in B-flat minor, Op. 35, even people who have never heard the rest of the piece. That slow, heavy tread has been played at state funerals and borrowed by cartoons. But the march is the third of four movements, and it means much more when you hear what comes before it and what comes after. I have performed this sonata, and I want to give you a map for listening to it as a whole.

First movement: a storm with a song inside it

The sonata opens with four slow bars marked Grave, a dark, questioning gesture in the bass. Then the music breaks into doppio movimento, double the speed, with an agitated, breathless figure that seems to run without ever arriving. Listen to the left hand here. It is restless, pushing the music forward like a pulse that is too fast.

Then, in the major key, a second theme appears: a broad, singing melody marked sostenuto. This is the contrast to hold on to. The whole movement is a struggle between that nervous energy and that long, calm song. Chopin does something unusual when the opening material returns at the end. The agitated first theme does not come back as you might expect; the music goes straight to the singing second theme, now in B-flat major. Listen for that moment. It changes the ending of the movement from a repetition into a kind of answer.

Second movement: the scherzo

The scherzo in E-flat minor is fierce and physical, built on repeated chords and leaping octaves that hammer and rebound. It is one of the most demanding passages in the sonata for the hands. Listen for how the rhythm drives everything and how the leaps create a sense of danger.

In the middle comes a slower section in G-flat major, a simple, almost innocent melody that floats above the texture. After all that hammering, it sounds like a memory of something gentle. When the fierce music returns, the contrast makes it even darker, and the movement ends by recalling the gentle melody one last time.

Third movement: the funeral march

The march is built on two chords rocking back and forth in the left hand, low and steady, like footsteps that cannot speed up. Above them, the melody moves very little. It is almost stuck on a few notes. That restraint is where its power comes from.

The middle section moves to D-flat major and gives us one of Chopin’s most beautiful melodies, long and quiet over a gently flowing accompaniment. The pianist’s job is to make this sound like a different world, not a louder or faster one. Then the march returns. Listen to how the same footsteps feel heavier the second time, because now you know what was lost.

Fourth movement: the finale

This is the strangest movement Chopin ever placed in a sonata. It lasts little more than a minute. Both hands play in unison, an octave apart, in a rapid, whispered stream of notes marked sotto voce and legato. There is no melody in the usual sense and almost no clear harmony. It sounds like wind, or like something passing over a grave after the mourners have gone.

Listen for how quiet it stays, and how the shape of the line suggests harmonies that are never fully stated. Then the sonata ends with one sudden, loud chord. After everything, that single chord feels like a door closing.

What this asks of the pianist

The difficulty of this sonata is not only the speed of the scherzo or the evenness of the finale. It is keeping four very different movements inside one arc. The pianist has to plan the sound so that the march is not simply loud and the finale is not simply fast. Each movement has to prepare the next.

What you can try this week

  • Listen to the whole sonata once without stopping. Do not look anything up. Notice where you feel tension and where you feel relief.
  • Listen to the funeral march again and count how many times the left-hand chords change in the first section. You will find the answer is very few. Then notice how the melody uses that steady ground.
  • At your own piano, play two low chords slowly, back and forth, and try to make them sound like steady footsteps: same weight, same timing, no accents. It is harder than it sounds.
  • Play a single melody note very softly over them and hold it. Listen to how long the sound lasts. That listening is the beginning of playing a slow melody well.

This sonata rewards patience. The more often you hear it, the more you realize that the famous march is the center of a story, not the whole of it.
