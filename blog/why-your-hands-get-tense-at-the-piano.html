Why Your Hands Get Tense at the Piano — and How to Stop

Adult beginners blame stiff hands and their age. It is almost always tension, it has three specific causes, and a concert pianist explains how to undo each one.
By Dr. Maria Pisarenko, DMA  ·  September 8, 2026

Watch the technique taught in full — free: the beginner course, Lesson 1: Open the free course →

Almost every adult I teach arrives with the same physical story, and almost none of them realize it is a story about the piano. Their shoulders creep up within a minute of starting. Their wrists lock. After twenty minutes their forearms ache, and they conclude — quietly, and quite wrongly — that they have started too late and their hands are simply too old for this.

Their hands are fine. What they have is a technique problem that no one has ever named for them, and it is one of the few problems in piano playing that can be fixed faster than it was acquired.

Tension is not effort. It is effort that never stopped.

Here is the mechanical fact that changes everything, and it is worth reading twice. Inside the piano, a felt hammer is thrown at a string. By the time the string sounds, the hammer is already flying free — it has left the mechanism behind. The only thing you ever control is how fast that hammer is traveling at the moment it lets go.

Which means that once you hear a note, your work on it is finished. You cannot press it louder. You cannot lean into it and make it grow. Everything you do to the key after the sound begins is muscular effort that produces no music whatsoever.

And that is exactly what tension is: work that continues after it has stopped being useful. Not too much strength — strength applied a fraction of a second too long, over and over, several hundred times in a practice session.

Where it comes from, and why the usual advice fails

People are usually told to relax. It is well meant and it does not work, because relaxation is not a thing you can do on command any more than you can decide to fall asleep. You cannot relax a muscle by wanting to. You can only give it something else to do, or stop asking it to work.

There are three habits behind most adult tension, and each has a specific replacement.

Pushing instead of dropping. If you press a key with your finger while your arm is braced above it, the arm has to hold itself up — a genuine, continuous muscular job — while the finger does the pressing. Two muscle groups working, one of them for nothing. Instead: lift the whole arm slightly, let it fall, and let its weight carry the finger into the key. The moment the sound begins, stop working. The finger holds the key down; nothing else holds anything.

Staying in one place. Many beginner methods put the hand in a fixed five-key position and leave it there for weeks. The hand learns to reach sideways with the fingers rather than travel with the arm, and the torso learns to stay perfectly still. A body held still for twenty minutes is a body doing static muscular work, which is precisely the kind that aches. Move. Take a short phrase and play it in three different octaves, leaning your whole body slightly toward each one. It feels like a trivial exercise. It is the antidote to an entire category of injury.

Playing faster than you can hear. When the music outruns your ear, the hand starts bracing in anticipation of notes it is not sure about. Every uncertain passage you have ever played fast is stored in your arms as a small permanent flinch. Slowing down until you can hear each note arrive is not a patience exercise. It removes the reason for the flinch.

The test that takes ten seconds

Play a single note. Drop the arm, let the sound begin, and then, without lifting the key, let everything above the fingertip go completely slack — forearm, elbow, shoulder. If the note keeps ringing, you have released correctly, and you have just felt the sensation the whole of piano technique is built on.

Now do it the other way: press the same key firmly with the finger and keep the arm braced. Listen to the front of the sound. There is a small knock in it, and the note dies sooner.

Once you have heard that difference in your own hands, you cannot un-hear it. Most adults find it within a few minutes, and it reorganises their playing more than any amount of finger exercise.

What to do this week

Five minutes at the start of each practice session, before anything else:

Drop your arm into five different keys, in five different parts of the keyboard, one at a time. After each one, hold the key down and follow the sound all the way out until there is genuinely nothing left. Do not lift early. The listening is the exercise; the dropping is only how you start it.

Then play whatever you are working on at half speed, with one instruction in mind: after each note, is the arm resting or is it still working? You will catch yourself perhaps every fourth note in the first week. By the third week you will catch yourself rarely, because the habit will largely be gone.

A word about age

Adults are more prone to this than children, and it is not because their hands are stiffer. It is because they arrive with desk-work shoulders, they practice without supervision, and they are conscientious — they try hard, and trying hard at the piano is very often the problem rather than the solution. The technique that fixes it is not a beginner's simplification. It is the same technique I teach to advanced students, because there is only one.

If you have been wondering whether your hands are the reason it feels difficult, they almost certainly are not. Give the release two weeks of honest attention and see what changes.
