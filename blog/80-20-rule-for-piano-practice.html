The 80/20 Rule for Piano Practice: What Actually Moves You

A few practice habits give most of the progress. Here are the five I would keep if I could keep only five, and the things I would stop doing.
By Dr. Maria Pisarenko, DMA  ·  February 1, 2027

Forty-one lessons in order, free, at your own pace: Start at Lesson 1 →

The 80/20 rule for piano practice says that a few habits give most of your progress. I want to be plain about one thing first: 80/20 is a rule of thumb, not a measurement. I cannot tell you that these habits produce eighty percent of anything, and I will not invent a number. What I can tell you, from teaching adults, is which habits matter most and which you can stop worrying about.

What is the 80/20 rule for learning piano?

The idea behind 80/20 is that a small share of causes often produces most of an effect. Applied to practice, it is a way of choosing, and choosing is exactly what a busy adult needs. You have twenty minutes, perhaps, not two hours, and the question is where the twenty minutes should go. The answer is not more minutes. It is a method, and the five habits below are the method in its shortest form. None of them requires talent, a special book or an expensive instrument, and all of them can start tonight.

I have taught students who practice forty minutes a day and progress slowly, and students who practice fifteen and progress quickly. The difference is rarely talent and rarely discipline. It is what happens during the minutes. My full account of that is in how to practice piano: a method, not just more minutes.

The five habits that carry most of the progress

1. Diagnose, do not repeat

When something goes wrong, stop and ask what went wrong, specifically. Not "I keep messing this up," but: my fourth finger is late, or my hand is still in the old position when the new note arrives. Almost every persistent mistake has one identifiable cause, and once it is named it usually takes a few minutes to fix. Left unnamed, it survives months of daily repetition. Adults are very good at this kind of diagnosis, far better than children, and it is one of the real advantages of starting later.

2. Slow enough to be correct

Never play a passage faster than you can play it correctly. If it goes wrong at speed, the speed is the error, not the passage. Slow down until it is easy, not until it is merely possible. At the speed where it is merely possible you are rehearsing your own unsteadiness, and at the speed where it is easy you are rehearsing accuracy, which is what gets faster on its own.

3. Hands separately

When two hands struggle together, you cannot tell which one is the problem, and neither can your brain. Separately, each hand can go faster, be listened to properly, and be fixed. Put them together only when each is comfortable, and expect to go back to separate hands several times. That is not a step backward. It is how the work is done.

4. Count out loud

Rhythm, not only the right notes, is what makes playing sound musical. Counting out loud is the cheapest way to fix it. It feels awkward for about three days, and then it holds your rhythm steady in a way that counting in your head does not, because your voice keeps the pulse going when your hands are busy. How to count rhythm out loud has the order I use.

5. Play through, for pleasure

End each session by playing something you can already play, all the way through, mistakes allowed. This is not wasted time. It is what makes you come back tomorrow, and coming back tomorrow is the entire game. The session should end with music, not with a problem.

What sits in the other 80

These are the things that feel like practice and often do very little:

  • Playing a piece from the top every time. The opening becomes beautiful and the middle never becomes anything at all. Begin at the difficult place while you are fresh.
  • Repeating without changing anything. Playing something ten times is not practice unless something changed between attempts. Every time you play a passage you cannot yet do, you are practicing doing it badly.
  • Counting minutes. Twenty focused minutes beat two distracted hours, and six short sessions beat one long weekend marathon, because skills consolidate between sessions.
  • Collecting methods. Adults often buy three method books and finish none. Choose one good book and work through it in order.

I do not mean these are worthless. Playing through a piece is a pleasure, and I am all for it. I mean they are not where the progress comes from.

The five habits in twenty minutes

Here is how the habits fit into one session. The shape is the same one I describe in how to practice piano with a busy schedule.

Three minutes, the body. Set the bench so your forearm is level with the tops of the keys, then play one note, letting the weight of the arm fall through a firm fingertip and releasing. Listen until the note disappears.

Eight minutes, the hard bar. Not the piece, the bar. Find the one place that breaks and take the two bars around it. Diagnose it, play it slowly enough that it cannot go wrong, hands separately if it is a two-hand problem, and count out loud. Five clean repetitions. If the sixth goes wrong, you were going too fast.

Six minutes, one new thing. The next page or the next idea, small and deliberate. One new thing per session is a good rate. Two is usually none.

Three minutes, play through. Something you already play, for pleasure.

If the day collapses, cut the new thing first. At five minutes, keep only the single-note exercise and one slow pass of your hard bar. That is a legitimate practice.

A week built on the rule

Six short sessions a week will do more than one long one, because the hand files what it learned while you sleep. Give five of those sessions the structure above. Give the sixth to a different job: play your piece through twice, and film one minute of it on your phone. Watch it that evening, with the sound on, and write down one thing you noticed. It is the cheapest teacher in the world, and it is the habit that most adults skip. Then use the one thing you noticed to choose the hard bar for the coming week.

That is the whole plan. It does not need a special book, an app or a bigger block of time. It needs the same few habits, in the same order, most days. If you take only one sentence from this article, take this one: the difference between students is rarely talent and rarely discipline. It is method, and method can be learned in a single afternoon.

How to know it is working

You will not feel a percentage. You will notice that a line of music takes less time to read, that your hands begin to do what you ask, and that a bar which failed last week now goes right on the second try. Progress arrives in plateaus and sudden jumps, and the plateau is where the learning is being consolidated. Please do not quit during it. For a longer view, see how long you should practice piano each day.

The one thing five habits cannot give you is another pair of ears. You cannot easily tell whether your own third finger is collapsing, because you hear what you meant to play. Questions adults ask

What is the 80/20 rule for piano practice?

It is a rule of thumb that a few habits give most of your progress. It is not a measurement, and I will not give you a percentage. The habits I would keep are diagnosing instead of repeating, practicing slowly enough to be correct, working hands separately, counting out loud and finishing by playing something through for pleasure.

How long should I practice piano each day to make progress?

For an adult beginner, 20 to 30 minutes of concentrated practice, most days, is enough to make real, visible progress. Six short sessions beat one long weekend marathon, because skills consolidate between sessions, during rest and sleep.

What should I cut when I only have five minutes?

Cut the new thing first. At five minutes, keep only the single-note weight exercise and one slow pass of your hard bar. That is a legitimate practice, even though it will not feel like one.
