What the Russian School Does Differently — and Why It Matters to an American Adult Beginner

I was trained in Moscow and I have taught in American universities for years. The two traditions are not better and worse. They are strong in different places, and an adult learning alone needs to know where.
By Dr. Maria Pisarenko, DMA  ·  September 14, 2026

The free course, built on exactly this — the American sequence with the Russian sound on top: Start at Lesson 1 →

I was taught at the Central Music School of the Moscow Conservatory and at the Gnessins Academy. I took my doctorate in the United States, and I have taught American students at university level ever since. So I have spent my life inside both traditions, and I am asked about the difference constantly — usually by adults who have bought a method book, got some way into it, and sense that something is missing without being able to name it.

Let me name it, and let me be fair to both, because the honest answer is not that one is right.

What the American method book is genuinely excellent at

Sequence. Nobody organizes a beginner's first year better. One new idea per page, titled with the name of the idea, and a familiar tune arriving a page or two later as the reward for having learned it. More than half the pieces in a typical American book are melodies an adult already knows, and that matters enormously, because a beginner who recognizes what they are playing practices more.

Alfred's Basic Adult All-in-One — the book my own free course follows, page by page — is a very good piece of engineering. It gets an adult playing something recognizable in the first two weeks, and it never asks two new things at once. I would not have built forty-one lessons on it if I did not respect it.

What it does not contain, and cannot

How the sound is made.

A method book can tell you which key to press and when. It cannot tell you whether your arm is hanging or braced, whether the sound came from your fingertip alone or from the weight of your whole arm behind it, or whether your wrist locked at the moment the passage got difficult. It cannot hear you. And these are not refinements for later — they are what the difference between a pleasant sound and a hard, thin one is made of, from the very first note.

In the Russian tradition that is not a separate topic. It is the subject. A child in Moscow spends weeks before ever reading a note: listening, singing the tune, then finding it with one finger, non-legato, feeling the key go down with the weight of the arm rather than the effort of the finger. Reading comes after the body already knows what a good sound feels like.

American books mostly cannot do this, and it is not a failing of the authors. It is because the method book was never meant to work alone. It supplies the sequence and the tunes; the teacher standing beside the piano supplies the sound. Every American conservatory teacher I know does exactly this — they teach from a method book and add the physical training on top.

Why this hits adults harder than children

A child has a teacher every week who simply takes their wrist and lifts it. An adult in America very often has no teacher at all. They have a book, a screen, and the assumption that what is printed is the whole of it.

So the gap lands entirely on them, and it shows up in three complaints I hear constantly:

  • “My hands ache after twenty minutes.” Almost never age. Almost always a wrist sitting below the keyboard and fingers doing work the arm should be doing.
  • “It sounds hard and thin, not like when you play it.” Correct, and it is not the instrument. It is that the sound is being produced by pressing rather than by weight and release.
  • “I can play it slowly but it falls apart at speed.” Usually a hand that has been held in one place from lesson one and never learned to travel.

None of those are reading problems. Every one of them is a sound-and-body problem, which is precisely the part the book does not contain.

Three things the Russian school does that you can do alone, today

You do not need to abandon your method book. Do these alongside it.

1. Hum it before you play it. Twice, all the way through, before the hands touch a key. If you cannot hum it, you do not yet know what you are trying to play, and your fingers will be guessing. This one habit changes more than anything else on this page.

2. Let the arm play, not the finger. Sit so your forearm is level with the keys or very slightly above — never with the wrist dropped below them. Then let a single note sound by allowing the arm's weight down through a firm fingertip, and release immediately after. You are listening for a sound that is round and carries, rather than short and hard. Do this for two minutes a day on one note, and you will begin to hear the difference in everything else.

3. Move the hand from the first week. Play the same three-note tune starting in three different places on the keyboard. Method books keep the hand in one fixed position for many pages because it simplifies the printing. The cost is a reader who reads finger numbers instead of notes, and a hand that panics when it finally has to travel.

So which should an American adult use

Both, and in this order: the American book for what to learn next, the Russian school for how to produce the sound while you learn it. That is not a compromise; it is what any good teacher does in the room. The book supplies the map. The tradition supplies the touch.

What an adult learning alone is missing is not a better book. It is the second half — and the only reason it feels unreachable is that nobody is standing next to the piano saying lift your wrist, listen to that note, do it again.

That is the gap my free course was built to close, and it is why I read and answer recordings that people send me. You can learn the notes from a page. You cannot hear yourself the way another musician hears you.
