How to Read Sheet Music: The Staff, Treble Clef & Your First Rhythm

By Dr. Maria Pisarenko, DMA · September 3, 2026

Watch this taught in full — free: Lesson 2 — Reading the Treble Staff · Lesson 3 — Rhythm & Note Values

Every adult who sits down at a piano for the first time hits the same wall within the first ten minutes: the page. Five lines, a curling symbol at the left edge, a stack of numbers, and a scatter of black dots that are somehow supposed to tell your hands what to do. It looks like a code, and most method books hand it to you as one — memorize the symbols, move on.

I teach it differently, because I don't think it's a code. It's a set of very old, very practical shortcuts, and once you know what each one is actually doing, none of them are mysterious.

The staff is just a map of "up" and "down"

The five lines and four spaces of a musical staff exist for one reason: to show you, at a glance, whether the next note is higher or lower than the one before it. That's the entire idea. A note sitting higher on the page is a higher pitch. A note sitting lower is a lower pitch. Everything else — clefs, key signatures, accidentals — is refinement layered on top of that one simple rule.

Why the treble clef looks the way it does

The curling symbol at the start of the staff is called a clef, and it isn't decoration — it's an anchor. The treble clef (sometimes called the G-clef) is a stylized letter G, and the curl of its lower loop wraps around the second line from the bottom of the staff. That line is G above middle C. Everything else on the staff is counted from there.

Knowing that changes how you should draw it, too. Start at the top, come down through the middle, and let the tail curl around that second line on purpose — not as an afterthought once the shape "looks right." Students who learn to draw it starting from the G line read from it faster, because the note it's anchored to was never a mystery in the first place.

What those two stacked numbers actually mean

At the very start of a piece of music, right after the clef, you'll see two numbers stacked like a fraction — most commonly 4 over 4. This is the time signature, and it is not a fraction, even though it's drawn like one.

The top number tells you how many beats are in each measure. The bottom number tells you what kind of note counts as one beat. In 4/4 time, there are four beats per measure, and a quarter note gets one beat. That's it. Once you can count "1, 2, 3, 4" out loud at a steady pace, you already understand the time signature — the numbers are just telling you which note shape matches each spoken count.

Quarter notes, half notes, and the beat you can't skip

This is where most adult beginners go wrong, and it isn't a hands problem — it's a counting problem. A quarter note (a filled-in oval with a stem) gets one beat. A half note (an open oval with a stem) gets two. On paper that distinction looks trivial. At the piano, under your fingers, it's the difference between music that has a pulse and music that doesn't.

The fix is almost embarrassingly simple: count out loud while you play, every time, even after you think you don't need to anymore. "1" for the quarter note, "1-2" for the half note. Your fingers will follow your voice long before they can hold the timing on their own — and the habit of counting out loud, kept up past the point where it feels necessary, is the single biggest difference I see between students who read fluently within a few months and students who are still guessing a year in.

None of this requires talent — it requires the right first explanation

I have a Doctor of Musical Arts and I've spent my career on stage and in the classroom, and I can tell you honestly: nothing about reading music rewards natural gift over correct instruction. The staff, the clef, the time signature, and note values are a small, closed set of rules. Learn them in the right order, with a teacher who can actually play what they're teaching, and they stop being a code and start being obvious.
