Who Taught the Person Teaching You? Why Piano Lineage Matters

A piano teacher's lineage shows which ideas shaped them; I lay out both branches of mine, from Liszt and from Leonid Nikolayev, and offer adults a practical way to judge any teacher.
By Dr. Maria Pisarenko, DMA  ·  October 13, 2026

Taught by Dr. Maria Pisarenko, DMA — the complete beginner course is free: Start at Lesson 1 →

When adults begin piano, they usually ask about price, schedule and whether a teacher is patient. Rarely do they ask the question I consider most revealing: who taught you? It is not a matter of pedigree for its own sake. Teaching is handed down by hand, ear and example, and what your teacher received will shape what you receive.

Why lineage is more than a name on a wall

A piano teacher does not invent a way of teaching from nothing. Everyone teaches, consciously or not, the way they were taught: how to sit, how to make a sound, what to listen for, what to forgive and what never to forgive. A line of teachers is a line of habits and standards. When you know the line, you can often predict the lesson.

That is also why I am wary of anyone who treats lineage as a trophy. A famous name five generations back guarantees nothing. What matters is whether the teacher can show you what they inherited, in sound, in your lesson, today.

The first branch: from Liszt to the Central Music School

My formative teacher at the Central Music School in Moscow was Manana Kandelaki, a pupil of Tatiana Nikolayeva. Tatiana Nikolayeva studied with Alexander Goldenweiser. Goldenweiser studied with Alexander Siloti, and Siloti studied with Franz Liszt. During those years I also studied regularly with Ilya Romanovich Klyachko, who was likewise a pupil of Alexander Goldenweiser.

  • Franz Liszt
  • Alexander Siloti
  • Alexander Goldenweiser
  • Tatiana Nikolayeva and Ilya Romanovich Klyachko
  • Manana Kandelaki
  • Maria Pisarenko

As a student I also played for Tatiana Nikolayeva herself, an experience no chart can convey. What it taught me was seriousness: music is something you owe your full attention.

The second branch: from Leonid Nikolayev to Gnessin

At the Gnessin Russian Academy of Music, my teacher was Yuri Vladimirovich Ponizovkin, a pupil of Maria Yudina and a scholar of Rachmaninoff. Maria Yudina, in turn, studied with Leonid Nikolayev at the Petrograd (later Leningrad) Conservatory. His pupils included Yudina, Vladimir Sofronitsky and Dmitri Shostakovich.

Shostakovich dedicated his Piano Sonata No. 2 to Leonid Nikolayev, and he said that Leonid Nikolayev trained not simply pianists, but in the first place thinking musicians. I have taken that sentence as a compass. A thinking musician hears what she plays, knows why a phrase goes where it goes, and can explain a choice instead of merely repeating it.

  • Leonid Nikolayev
  • Maria Yudina
  • Yuri Vladimirovich Ponizovkin
  • Maria Pisarenko

Also at Gnessin, I learned from Dr. Lina Bulatova and Dr. Vera Nossina, and in lessons and masterclasses I worked with Eliso Virsaladze, Vera Gornostayeva, Sergei Dorensky, Lev Naumov and Vladimir Krainev.

What I took from all of it

Two branches, one shared conviction: sound comes first, and the mind must be awake while the fingers work. That is why my teaching rests on the Russian tradition of seeing a passage, hearing it in your head, and only then playing it, and why I ask adults to judge their progress by the sound they make rather than by watching their hands.

How an adult can judge a teacher, lineage or not

You do not need to memorize anyone’s family tree. You need to know whether the teacher’s training shows up in the lesson. Here is what I would look for:

  • Ask the question. “Who did you study with, and what did they teach you that you still use?” A good teacher answers specifically, with ideas, not only names.
  • Listen to them play. A teacher should be able to demonstrate what they ask of you. Their sound is the best credential there is.
  • Watch how they correct you. Do they explain why, so that you can fix the next problem alone? That is the thinking musician at work.
  • Notice what they listen to. Does the teacher respond to your sound, or only to whether the notes were right?
  • Check that they teach adults as adults. You are not a small child, and good teaching respects that you already think, read and reason.
  • Be wary of promises. No one can promise how fast you will progress. A serious teacher promises honest work and honest feedback.

If you want more on this decision, my guide to choosing a piano teacher goes through it step by step.

Why this matters to you

I spent my working life teaching adults who were sure it was too late. What I have found is that they are often the best students, because they ask why. A lineage built on thinking musicians suits exactly that kind of learner. You do not need a famous name behind your teacher. You need a teacher who can hand you, clearly and kindly, something real.
