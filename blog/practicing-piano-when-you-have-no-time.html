Practicing Piano When You Have a Job, a Family and Twenty Minutes

The commonest reason adults stop is not difficulty. It is time. So here is precisely what I would put in your twenty minutes, and what I would cut first.
By Dr. Maria Pisarenko, DMA  ·  September 18, 2026

The free course, built for exactly this kind of week — short lessons, in order: Start at Lesson 1 →

When an adult tells me they are thinking of stopping, they very rarely say the piano is too hard. They say there is no time. Work, children, a commute, a parent who needs looking after. The instrument sits there and the guilt attaches to it, and eventually the easiest way to stop feeling guilty is to stop looking at it.

I want to say something that professionals know and beginners are rarely told: twenty minutes is not a compromise. Twenty minutes, spent properly, is a real practice. Most of the damage is done not by short sessions but by unstructured ones — by an hour of playing a piece from the top, getting stuck in the same bar, going back to the top, and calling it practice.

Why short and daily beats long and occasional

Playing the piano is a physical skill before it is anything else, and physical skills consolidate while you sleep. Six twenty-minute sessions give your hands six nights to file what they learned. One two-hour Sunday gives them one. The arithmetic of total minutes says the two are similar; the arithmetic of learning says they are not remotely similar.

This is also why the very short practice is worth defending. A day you were sure was lost, rescued by five minutes at the keyboard, keeps the thread — and the thread, not the total, is what carries adults through their first year.

The twenty minutes, in order

Three minutes — the body. Before any music. Set the bench so your forearm is level with the tops of the keys. Then, on one single note, let the arm's weight fall through a firm fingertip, and release. Listen to the note until it disappears. Ten times, slowly, each hand. This is not a warm-up in the athletic sense; it is you reminding your arm who is doing the work, and it is the difference between a round sound and a hard one for the whole rest of the session.

Eight minutes — the hard bar. Not the piece. The bar. Find the one place that breaks, take the two bars around it, and play them slowly enough that they cannot go wrong. Hands separately first if it is a hands-together problem. Then the same passage, still slow, five times without an error — and if the sixth goes wrong, you were going too fast, so slow down rather than repeat. This is the part that adults skip, and it is the only part that actually changes anything.

Six minutes — the new thing. The next page, the next lesson, the next idea. Small and deliberate. One new thing per session is a genuinely good rate; two is usually none.

Three minutes — play something you can already play. All the way through, for pleasure, mistakes allowed. I insist on this with every student. You need to finish at the instrument feeling like a person who plays the piano, because that feeling is what brings you back tomorrow, and tomorrow is the entire strategy.

What to cut when the day collapses

Cut the new thing. Always cut the new thing first — it is the piece of the session that will be there unchanged tomorrow.

Then, at five minutes, keep only two things: the single-note weight exercise, and one slow pass of your hard bar. That is a legitimate practice. It will not feel like one. It is one.

What I would ask you never to cut is the hour. Not the length — the hour of the day. Adults who practice at a fixed time survive; adults who practice when a gap appears discover that gaps stop appearing. Before the coffee, after the children are down, whatever it is. The decision you are making is about a slot, not about an amount.

Two habits that quietly waste half of it

Starting from the top every time. If you always begin at bar one, the opening becomes beautiful and the middle never becomes anything at all. Begin at the difficult place while you are fresh.

Repeating rather than practicing. Playing something ten times is not practice unless something changed between the attempts. Say out loud, each time, what is different: slower, louder in the left hand, counting aloud, eyes on the page instead of the keys. Repetition without an instruction only makes the current version more permanent, including its mistakes.

And one honest warning

The thing twenty minutes cannot give you is another pair of ears. You can build a perfectly good practice at home; you cannot easily tell whether your own third finger is collapsing, because you hear what you meant to play. Once a week, put your phone on the music stand and film one minute of it. Watch it that evening. It is the cheapest teacher in the world.

And if you would like a musician to watch it with you, send it to me — I answer recordings myself and it costs nothing. Twenty honest minutes a day and one set of outside ears a week will take an adult beginner further in a year than most people manage in three.
