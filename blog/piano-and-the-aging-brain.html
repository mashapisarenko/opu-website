Does Playing Piano Keep Your Brain Sharp? What Research Says

Keeping the mind sharp is one of the best reasons to start. It is also the reason most often oversold. Here is what the studies show, and how to practice so the work counts.
By Dr. Maria Pisarenko, DMA  ·  October 19, 2026

Structured lessons, in order, free: Start at Lesson 1 →

"I want to keep my mind sharp." Adults give me this reason more often than almost any other, and after fifty it comes up in nearly every first conversation. It is a very good reason to sit down at the piano. It is also the reason that attracts the most exaggeration online, so before anything else I want to separate what researchers have actually found from what gets promised in advertisements.

Why the piano asks so much of the brain

Think about what happens in a single bar of easy music. Your eyes read two lines of notation at once. You translate each note into a key and a finger. Your two hands do different things at the same moment, and often at different speeds. You keep a steady count. You listen to the sound you just made and adjust the next one. And while all of that is happening, your eyes are already a beat or two ahead, preparing what comes next.

Very few activities combine reading, fine movement of ten fingers, timing, listening and planning in one continuous moment. That is exactly why neuroscientists find the piano so interesting, and why so many studies of learning in later life use it.

What the research has found

A year of piano lessons, compared with a year of listening. Research teams in Geneva and Hannover followed 156 healthy older adults who had never had musical training. They were randomly assigned either to piano lessons or to a music-listening course, and tested over a full year. The results, published in the European Journal of Neuroscience in 2023, showed that the piano group improved more in fine motor control, and probably in working memory, and that changes in the hands went together with changes in the motor areas of the brain. The authors put their conclusion plainly: playing an instrument "promotes motor, cognitive and neural development into older age."

Piano training and the thinking skills that organize everything else. As early as 2007, Jennifer Bugos and her colleagues reported that individualized piano instruction improved executive functioning and working memory in older adults. A randomized controlled trial published in The Journals of Gerontology in 2022, summarized by AARP, reported gains in working memory, processing speed and verbal fluency in adults between 60 and 80 after piano training.

Playing an instrument and dementia. A 2022 review in BMC Neurology pooled long-term studies and found that older adults who played a musical instrument were less likely to develop dementia. This is an association, not proof: people who play may differ from people who do not in many other ways. AARP's Global Council on Brain Health reached a similarly careful conclusion in 2020: making music is associated with better attention, thinking and mood.

What this does and does not mean

It does not mean the piano is a medicine, or that it can prevent any illness. Nobody can honestly promise that, and I will not.

It does mean that learning the piano is one of the most complete workouts a healthy adult brain can be given, and that the benefits measured in these studies came from learning — from structured, progressive lessons in which something new and slightly difficult appeared every week. Playing the same three pieces from memory every evening is a pleasure, and I am all for it. But it is not the part of piano that asks the brain to grow.

How to practice if your brain is part of the reason

Read the music; do not only copy it. Reading is the most demanding part of playing, and it is the part that self-taught adults most often skip by copying hands from a video. If your reason is cognitive, reading is where the work is. Start with the grand staff, explained properly, and if you have been stuck for a while, read why reading stays slow and what fixes it.

Let the hands become independent gradually. Two hands doing two different things is the exact skill that challenges coordination and attention. It does not come by force; it comes by a sequence of small steps, which I explain in why your hands will not do two things at once.

Memorize short pieces. Memorizing music uses several kinds of memory at once: the sound, the hand movement, the picture of the page and the structure of the harmony. The four kinds of memory shows how to use all of them.

Keep something new on the music stand. A new piece, a new key, a new rhythm. Novelty is what the brain responds to. A structured course takes care of this for you, because each lesson adds one thing you have not done before.

Practice a little every day. Twenty or thirty minutes most days does more than two hours once a week, because the brain consolidates what it learned between sessions. If time is the obstacle, this is how to practice with twenty minutes.

Questions adults ask me about this

Is it too late for the benefits? The people in the Geneva and Hannover study were older adults who had never played an instrument, and they still changed over a single year. The brain stays able to learn throughout life. It learns a little more slowly than a child's, and a sensible plan simply allows for that.

Does listening to music count? Listening is a pleasure and good for the mood, and I would never discourage it. But in that study, listening was the comparison: the group that learned to play improved more in fine motor control than the group that listened. Making music asks more of the brain than hearing it.

Can I teach myself, or do I need structure? The people in these studies followed structured instruction, with something new each week in a sensible order. That structure is what guarantees the brain keeps meeting new challenges at the right size. A course that builds one skill at a time gives you that; a random collection of tutorials rarely does.

How soon will I notice anything? I will not give you a number for your brain. What you will notice within weeks is easier to describe: a line of music takes less time to read, and your hands begin to do what you ask of them. That is learning, and you can see it happening.

The part the studies cannot measure

Working memory and processing speed can be tested in a laboratory. What cannot be tested is the feeling of sitting down in the evening and playing Moon River for the first time from the page, with both hands, for someone you love. Adults who come to the piano for their brain usually stay for the music.
