Learning Piano With Arthritis or Stiff Hands

Stiff hands change how you practice, not whether you can play. Here is how to work with them instead of against them.
By Dr. Maria Pisarenko, DMA  ·  October 22, 2026

Gentle, unhurried lessons you can pause: Start at Lesson 1 →

"Can I still learn with my hands the way they are?" Adults in their sixties and seventies ask me this, often while looking at their knuckles. Some have arthritis; many simply have hands that are stiffer in the morning than they used to be.

First, the honest part: I am a pianist, not a physician. If you have arthritis, talk to your doctor before you begin, and if you can, see a hand therapist; they can tell you what movements suit your particular joints. What I can tell you is how pianists protect their hands, because good piano technique is, above all, the art of playing without strain. Those principles help stiff hands more than anyone.

Warm hands play better

Cold, stiff joints and a difficult passage are a poor combination. Before you sit down, hold your hands in warm water for a minute or two, or wrap them around a warm mug. Then begin with slow, easy playing: single notes, a few gentle five-finger patterns, nothing fast and nothing wide. The first five minutes of every session belong to the hands, not to the piece. A five-minute warm-up that is actually worth doing shows exactly what to play.

Weight, not force

The most important thing I teach any adult is that the sound comes from the weight of the arm dropping into the key, not from the finger pushing. For stiff hands this is not a detail; it is the whole approach. A finger that pushes works its small joints hard. A finger that simply transmits the arm's weight barely works at all.

Try this: let your arm hang at your side, completely loose. Lift it, place a slightly curved finger on a key, and let the arm's weight sink through it. The key goes down. You did not press. That is the touch to build everything on.

Curved, but never clawed

The hand should be gently rounded, as if resting on a ball. It should not be gripped into a claw. Let the fingers be as curved as is comfortable for your joints, not as curved as a picture in a book. Why curved fingers matter explains the principle; your comfort sets the degree.

Choose the instrument for your hands

The weight of the keys matters. Many digital pianos let you adjust touch sensitivity, so that a lighter stroke produces a full sound. Try several instruments in a store if you can, and choose one whose keys feel comfortable to play for ten minutes, not just for one chord. The right keyboard, light and music covers the rest of the setup, and the bench matters too: here is how to set its height.

Fingering is yours to adjust

Printed fingering is a recommendation for an average hand. If a finger is sore or a stretch is uncomfortable, change it. Use a stronger finger, shift the hand instead of stretching, or share a passage between the hands. Pianists do this constantly; the music only cares that the right note sounds well.

Avoid wide stretches, at least at first

Octaves and large chords put the most strain on the hand. Early on, choose music that stays within a five-note position. When a chord is too wide, roll it gently from bottom to top instead of grabbing all the notes at once. It is a perfectly musical solution, and many great pianists with smaller hands have used it.

Short sessions, more often

Three ten-minute sessions spread through the day are kinder to stiff joints than one half hour, and the brain learns just as well from them. Stop before the hands are tired, not after.

Music that suits stiff hands

Some music is kind to the joints and some is not. Slow, lyrical pieces, where the melody moves from note to neighboring note and the hand stays in one area of the keyboard, are ideal. Fast repeated notes, octaves played one after another, and wide leaps put the most strain on the hand, so leave them for later or for never; there is more beautiful music without them than anyone could play in a lifetime.

When you choose a goal piece, play through the first page slowly before you commit to it. If any spot asks your hand to do something uncomfortable, it is fine to choose a different arrangement, or a different piece. How to choose a piece you will actually finish has more on this.

And if you send me a recording, tell me about your hands. It changes the advice I give.

The pain rule

A mild feeling of having worked, like after a walk, is normal. Sharp pain, pain that lingers into the next day, numbness or tingling are not; stop, and speak to your doctor. Never play through pain to finish a piece. What hand pain at the piano means describes the difference in more detail.

On difficult days, practice without the keys

On days when the hands say no, the music does not have to stop. Read through tomorrow's piece and name the notes. Listen to a good recording of it with the score in front of you. Tap the rhythm lightly on your knee. Play it in your mind, hearing each note. This is real practice, and it makes the next day at the keyboard noticeably easier.

What the piano gives back

Many adults find that gentle, regular playing becomes one of the things that keeps their hands moving. I cannot promise that it will help your joints, and I will not. I can promise that stiff hands do not shut you out of music. They ask you to play the way the best pianists play anyway: with weight instead of force, with patience, and with care.
