Learning Piano as an Adult: Whatever Your Reason, Start Here

By Dr. Maria Pisarenko, DMA · August 26, 2026

Watch this taught in full — free: Lesson 1 - Warm-Up, Posture & the Keys

Adults come to the piano for very different reasons. Over the years I've taught people who arrived for almost every one of them, and I've noticed something: the reason shapes how you should practise, but it doesn't change what you need at the start.

See which of these sounds like you.

"I want something calming after work"

This is the most common reason adults give me, and it's a good one. Playing occupies your hands, your ears and your attention completely — there's no room left for the day you've just had. Many students describe it as the only twenty minutes when their mind is genuinely quiet.

If this is you: keep sessions short and regular rather than long and occasional, and always end with something you can already play, purely for pleasure. Don't turn your calm hour into another performance review. Progress will come anyway.

"I want to keep my mind sharp"

Piano is unusually demanding cognitively, in the best way. You're reading two lines of notation at once, translating them into two different hand movements, keeping time, listening critically and planning ahead — simultaneously. Very few activities ask that much of your attention, coordination and memory at the same time.

Students who come for this reason often do beautifully, because they're patient with the process and genuinely enjoy the puzzle. If this is you: lean into reading music and memorising short pieces — those are the parts that work your mind hardest.

"I always wanted to, and never got the chance"

Perhaps there was no instrument at home, or no money for lessons, or life simply went another way. I hear this constantly, usually with an apology attached — as though wanting it now needs excusing.

It doesn't. Adults have real advantages: you chose this yourself, you can understand explanations a child can't, you can analyse a problem, and you'll practise because you want to rather than because someone made you. What you have less of is time — which is exactly why it matters to learn correctly from the beginning rather than wandering for a year.

"I played as a child and stopped"

Returners are a joy to teach, and they're often surprised by how much is still in their hands. Muscle memory is remarkably durable.

One honest warning: your ear usually remembers more than your fingers do, so you'll hear exactly how it should sound while your hands lag behind. That gap is frustrating, and it's the reason most returners quit again in the first month. It closes faster than you think — but it does need those first weeks of patience.

"I just want to play one particular piece"

A wedding song. A piece your father used to play. Für Elise, because it's Für Elise. This is a perfectly legitimate goal, and I'd never talk anyone out of it.

The route matters, though. Learning that one piece purely by copying a tutorial — key by key, with no understanding of what you're doing — gets you one piece and nothing else. Learn a small amount of foundation first, and you get that piece plus the ability to learn the next one on your own. It's usually only a few extra weeks, and it changes everything that follows.

What everyone needs, regardless

Whatever brought you here, the first things are the same for all of you: sit properly, shape your hand properly, find your way around the keyboard, count out loud, and go slowly enough to be accurate. These are not obstacles between you and the music — they are the music, in the sense that they determine what you'll be able to do with it.

And one more thing that applies to every single reason on this page: learn from someone who is actually qualified to teach you — someone with real training, real teaching experience, and video evidence that they can play. Your reason for learning is personal. The standard of teaching you deserve isn't negotiable.

Where to start today

Sit at the middle of the keyboard. Rub your hands warm, make a fist and snap your fingers open five times. Put your right thumb on middle C and play up — C, D, E, F, G — then back down, watching that your palm stays domed and you land on your fingertips.

That's a real beginning. It takes two minutes, and it's the same two minutes I start my university students with.
