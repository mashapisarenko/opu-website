How to Count Music Out Loud Without Losing Your Place

Counting is not a beginner's crutch you outgrow. It is the only way a pulse gets into the body, and there is a right way and a wrong way to do it.
By Dr. Maria Pisarenko, DMA  ·  September 14, 2026

Watch me teach this at the keyboard — free: Lesson 3 — Quarter and half notes, counting out loud →

Most adults who are learning the piano count in their heads, or count for the first bar and then stop, or do not count at all and hope the rhythm arrives by itself. Then something goes wrong in the middle of a piece and they cannot say where.

Counting out loud fixes this, and it is not a beginner's crutch to be outgrown. Professional musicians count. They count in rehearsal, they count when learning something difficult, and they count out loud when a passage will not settle. What changes with experience is not whether you count, but how much of it happens silently.

Why out loud, and not in your head

Because counting in your head uses the same attention you are already spending on the notes, and attention does not divide. The moment a chord is difficult, the silent counting stops — and it stops without your noticing, which is the dangerous part.

Your voice is separate. It runs on breath and muscle, not on the part of you that is reading the page, and once a count is in the voice it keeps going while the hands struggle. That is exactly what you want: a pulse that does not pause when the music gets hard.

There is also a plain physical reason. A count you say aloud is something you can hear. If the four beats of a bar come out uneven, you will hear it immediately, the same way you hear a wrong note. Counted silently, an uneven bar simply feels normal.

Count the beats, not the notes

This is where most beginners go wrong, and it is worth being exact about.

In four-four time you count one, two, three, four — four beats in the bar, always, regardless of how many notes are in it. A bar with one long note still gets four counts. A bar with four short notes gets the same four counts. The counting does not speed up because there are more notes and does not slow down because there are fewer.

A half note is not called “half” as a name. It is two beats, and you say “one, two” while the key stays down, and you do not lift until you have said the second number. Most cut-short long notes I hear from adults are not a misunderstanding of the symbol at all — the student knows perfectly well it is two beats. They simply stopped counting and moved on when the sound seemed long enough.

The order that actually works

Do not try to count and play at the same time on the first attempt. That is two new things at once, and something will give way. Take them in this order, on every new piece:

  1. Clap the rhythm and count out loud. No piano at all. Just the bar and your voice. If you cannot do it away from the instrument, you certainly cannot do it at the instrument.
  2. Play and say the note names. Still no counting — one thing at a time.
  3. Play and count. Now put them together, and slowly enough that neither one breaks.
  4. Play and say the note names again, once the rhythm is secure.

And before any of that, one step I take from the Russian school I was trained in: hum the piece through twice before your hands touch a key. If you cannot hum it, you do not yet know what you are trying to play, and the hands will be guessing.

When the counting and the hands come apart

They will, and it is not a sign of anything wrong with you. It means the tempo is faster than your current coordination, and there is only one honest fix.

Slow down until it is easy, not until it is possible. Those are different speeds, and the gap between them is where the practice actually happens. At the speed where it is merely possible you are rehearsing your own unsteadiness. At the speed where it is easy you are rehearsing accuracy, and accuracy is what gets faster on its own.

If it still comes apart, count one bar aloud before you start playing — an empty bar of “one, two, three, four” at the speed you intend. Most breakdowns happen because the first note arrived before any pulse existed.

What to count when the notes are shorter

When eighth notes arrive, do not start counting faster. The beats are still the beats. You fill the space between them instead: one and two and three and four and, with the “and” landing exactly halfway.

The word “and” is doing real work there. It is not filler — it marks the half-beat, which is why saying it aloud makes eighth notes even almost immediately, while thinking it does very little.

How long you keep doing this

Until the pulse is in your body rather than in your voice, which for most adult beginners is a few months, and never entirely.

The counting gets quieter, then becomes a whisper, then a feeling. But it comes back the moment you meet something hard, and it should. An experienced player who cannot immediately play a tricky bar does exactly what you are being asked to do now: they slow it down and count it out loud, and they are not embarrassed about it.

That is the real point. Counting is not the thing beginners do. It is the thing musicians do, and you are starting it early.
