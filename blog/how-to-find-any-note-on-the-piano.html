How to Find Any Note on the Piano — the Two-Black-Key Trick

Eighty-eight keys, one pattern, and a single landmark that makes the whole keyboard readable in about a minute. Piano note names for adult beginners.
By Dr. Maria Pisarenko, DMA  ·  September 9, 2026

Watch me do this at the keyboard — free: the beginner course, Lesson 1: Open the free course →

The keyboard is the least intimidating thing about the piano, and almost every beginner is intimidated by it. Eighty-eight keys look like eighty-eight separate things to memorize. They are not. There are twelve, repeated seven times, and there is one landmark that makes the whole layout readable in about a minute.

Look at the black keys, not the white ones

Ignore the white keys entirely for a moment and look only at the black ones. They are not evenly spaced. They come in alternating groups: two together, then three together, then two, then three, all the way up and all the way down.

That pattern is the map. Everything else follows from it, and once you have noticed it you cannot stop seeing it.

The one thing to memorize

C is the white key immediately to the left of every group of two black keys.

That is the whole trick. Not "the C nearest the middle", not "count up from the bottom" — every group of two black keys has a C sitting just to its left, and there are seven or eight of them on a full piano.

Go and find five different Cs on your instrument now, out loud, without counting anything. It should take you under a minute, and after a few days of doing it you will simply see them, the way you see a letter rather than working it out.

The rest of the alphabet, and there is no more of it

The white keys are named with the first seven letters — A, B, C, D, E, F, G — and then they start again. Names go up as you move right, down as you move left. There is no eighth letter and there is nothing irregular hiding further along the keyboard.

So once you have found a C, everything else is a short walk. The key to its right is D, then E, then F, then G, then A, then B, and then you are at the next C, standing to the left of the next group of two black keys.

A second landmark, if you want one: F is the white key immediately to the left of every group of three. Between C and F you can locate any white key on the instrument without counting from anywhere.

What about the black keys themselves

Each black key has two names, and this confuses beginners far more than it should. The black key just above C is called C sharp — a sharp means the next key up. The same black key is also called D flat, because a flat means the next key down, and that key is just below D.

Same key, two names. Which name gets used depends on the harmony around it, and that is a question for later. For now it is enough to know that the black keys are not extra notes you have not learned yet. They sit between the white ones, and you already know where they are, because you used them to find everything else.

Do not learn this from a diagram alone

Reading about the pattern takes two minutes. Owning it takes about a week of doing one small thing.

Each day, before you play anything else, spend two minutes naming keys out loud as you press them. Up one octave and back down. Slowly, and actually saying the names — silently does not work nearly as well, and I do not entirely know why, but every teacher will tell you the same.

Then find five Cs without counting. Then five Fs. That is three minutes in total.

Why this small thing matters so much later

Because in a few weeks you will start reading music, and reading is nothing more than turning a symbol on a page into a pitch under your hand. If, at that moment, your instinct is which finger is that? rather than which note is that?, reading will stay slow and effortful for years.

This is the real cost of methods that put your hand in a fixed position and have you follow finger numbers. They get you playing a tune quickly and they quietly postpone the moment you learn where you actually are. Adults who spend three minutes a day naming keys out loud in their first fortnight almost never have that problem, and they are usually reading comfortably at a point where others are still decoding.

It is the cheapest investment available to a beginner, and it happens to be the one that pays the longest.
