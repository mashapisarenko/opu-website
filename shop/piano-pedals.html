Why I recommend this gear

I recommend this gear because it is what my adult students need to work through my free 41-lesson course and, later, to use with my upcoming method books. I am a concert pianist and a university piano professor, and these are the kinds of tools I look at first when a student asks what to buy.

How I choose

  • Piano-style action, so the pedal moves and returns like a real one.
  • Compatibility. Universal polarity pedals are made to work with many keyboards; check your instrument's manual.
  • Half-pedal control if your piano supports it, for finer shading later on.
  • A non-slip base, so the pedal stays where your foot puts it.
  • A pedal unit if you want all three pedals on a digital piano.

How this connects to your learning

How this helps you learn: a pedal that behaves like a piano pedal teaches your foot the right movement from the start, so you will not have to relearn it when you play pieces from my upcoming method books.

Questions adults ask

Do I need a sustain pedal as a beginner?

You will need one sooner than you expect, because much classical writing assumes it. Start with a proper piano-style pedal so your foot learns the right movement.

Will any sustain pedal work with my keyboard?

Not always. Look for a pedal described as universal and read your keyboard's manual for pedal polarity and the jack type.

What is half-pedaling?

Pressing the pedal only part of the way down, for a partial effect. It needs a pedal and an instrument that support continuous control, which the Roland DP-10 listing describes.

Is a three-pedal unit worth it?

Only if your digital piano is built to accept one. It matters when you move toward the repertoire that uses the soft and middle pedals.
